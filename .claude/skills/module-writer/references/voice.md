# Voice and depth guide

## Who is talking

A practitioner with fifty years in the business who has lived through several cycles, made most of the expensive mistakes personally or watched friends make them, and now mentors exactly one person. Not a lecturer, not a guru, not a brand. Warm but unsentimental; patient with confusion, impatient with sloppiness.

Concretely:

- **Second person, direct address.** "You'll be tempted to...", "Here's what I'd do in your seat."
- **First-person experience, as composites.** "I've watched three different buyers..." / "Early in my career I..." Keep anecdotes short (3-6 sentences), specific (dollar amounts, dates as eras like "the spring after rates jumped"), and consequential (what it cost, what changed). Invent freely but never name real people or companies and never claim a fabricated anecdote is documented history.
- **Plain words first, jargon second.** Introduce the term the way a mentor would: "Lenders call this DSCR. Think of it as how many times the property can pay its own mortgage."
- **Calibrated confidence.** Strong opinions on things that are settled by experience ("never underwrite to the seller's pro forma"). Honest uncertainty where it exists ("rates will do what they do; I only care whether the deal survives both outcomes").
- **Dry humor is welcome; cleverness for its own sake is not.**

## What makes it "fifty years" and not "textbook"

Every lesson must contain, in some form:

1. **A cold open.** A story, a mistake, or a number that creates a question. Not "In this module we will learn."
2. **The mechanism explained through a number.** Never "cap rates are inversely related to value" without "$100,000 of NOI is worth $1.54M at a 6.5% cap and $1.33M at 7.5%. That one-point move erased $210,000 and the rent didn't change."
3. **The mistakes section.** Real, specific, ranked by how often and how badly. Not "failing to do due diligence" but "trusting a T-12 the seller's bookkeeper prepared three days before listing, with no bank statements behind it."
4. **Judgment calls the textbooks skip.** The situations where the formula says yes and the veteran says no (or the reverse): when to pay up, when to walk, who to call first, what you ask the seller's broker that reveals the truth, what the first sign of trouble looks like.
5. **Rules of thumb with their failure conditions.** A rule of thumb without "and here's when it breaks" is a trap. Give the rule, the typical range, and the case where it fails.
6. **People and incentives.** Who is on the other side of the table, what are they paid to do, what are they not telling you. Brokers, lenders, sellers, property managers, partners, agents, distributors, actors.
7. **A closer.** "If you remember nothing else" (3 bullets max) and "This week" (one concrete thing to do with a real property, listing, deal, script, or spreadsheet).

## Specificity standard

- Prefer a number to an adjective: "a typical turn runs $2,500-$4,500 and 10-18 days" beats "turns are expensive."
- Give ranges with context, not false precision: "Class B apartments in secondary Sunbelt metros traded roughly 5.25-6.5% cap rates in the mid-2020s."
- When a specific figure is illustrative, say so in passing ("call it $1,400 a month").
- Use round, checkable numbers in worked examples so the learner can follow the arithmetic and then rebuild it themselves.
- Include the sensitivity: after each worked example, change one input and show what happens. The mentor's real lesson is usually in the sensitivity, not the base case.

## Pacing and format of the lesson

- Use `##` subheadings every ~300-400 words; lesson is read on a screen and needs landmarks.
- Mix prose with the occasional table or short list, but the backbone is prose. A lesson that is mostly bullet points is a summary, not a mentor.
- Short paragraphs (2-5 sentences). Vary rhythm. One-sentence paragraphs for emphasis, used sparingly.
- Bold sparingly for a term being defined or for a rule you want remembered.
- A `>` blockquote can set off a rule of thumb or a line you'd say out loud ("> Never fall in love with the pro forma.").

## Worked example anatomy

Each example is a self-contained scenario. Use this skeleton (headings as `##` inside the example):

1. `## The setup`: who, what, asking price/budget/situation, in 1-2 paragraphs.
2. `## The numbers`: one or two tables. Every figure derivable from the inputs. Show formulas in prose underneath ("$104,000 / 0.065 = $1,600,000").
3. `## What the veteran sees`: the read-through. What the numbers say, what they hide, what you'd do next and why.
4. `## Change one thing`: a sensitivity or a "what if it goes wrong" with recomputed numbers.

Examples within a module should differ in kind, not just in inputs: e.g., one clean base case, one that reveals a hidden problem, one that is a close call where judgment decides.

## Quiz writing

- **Multiple choice** tests a concept with a trap: the wrong options are the tempting misreadings (using NOI instead of cash flow, forgetting reserves, confusing rate with yield).
- **Scenario** gives 4-8 sentences of situation with numbers and asks for the best next move or the best read. It should require applying the lesson, ideally with a calculation the learner can do on paper in under two minutes.
- At least one question per module tests a **judgment call** (no arithmetic answer; the best answer is a decision with a reason).
- 3-4 options per question. Every option has a `::` explanation, including the correct one (why it's right *and* what it costs or assumes). Wrong-option explanations should name the specific thinking error, kindly.
- The takeaway (`>` lines) is what the mentor says after the student answers: the principle in one or two sentences, not a restatement of the answer.
- Mix the correct answer's length, style, and detail. Avoid the pattern where the right answer is the most hedged, longest, or most "reasonable-sounding."
- Never write "all of the above," "none of the above," or "both A and B." Never refer to options by letter; the app shuffles them.

## Failure modes (check the draft against these)

- **Generic filler**: "It's important to note," "In today's market," "delve," "navigate the landscape," "in conclusion." Delete.
- **Definition dump**: the lesson lists terms with one-line definitions. Replace each with a mechanism and a number.
- **Perfect examples**: every worked example works out cleanly. Real deals have a hidden problem; at least one example per module should surface one.
- **Number-free confidence**: strong claims with no figures. Add the figure or soften the claim.
- **Disclaimer wallpaper**: boilerplate "consult a professional" every paragraph. Instead, say once, in voice, exactly *which* professional and *what* to ask them.
- **Quiz that tests recall**: "What does NOI stand for?" Replace with something that requires applying it.
- **Over-uniform structure**: every lesson with the same five headings in the same order. Keep the required parts but let the story dictate the section titles.
- **Inconsistent math**: a figure quoted in the lesson that disagrees with the example table. Re-run the scratch script and grep the module for each number.
