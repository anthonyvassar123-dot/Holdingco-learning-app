# Lesson

The best exit I ever made, I made nine months early, and I almost didn't. I owned a 60-unit building I'd bought, renovated, and stabilized, and a broker called to say a buyer would pay a price that was a point lower in cap rate than anything I'd underwritten. My model said to hold for three more years. My gut said the building still had juice. My partner said we'd never get that price again. I made myself do one boring exercise: I calculated what my equity was earning if I kept holding it, compared to what that equity could earn elsewhere. It was earning about 7%. I knew I could find 12%. We sold. A year later rates rose, cap rates widened, and a building just like mine in the same town traded for 18% less.

The worst exit I ever made went the other way. I sold a building because the loan was coming due and I was tired, took a price I'd have laughed at two years earlier, and watched the buyer, who had a better lender and more patience, make a fortune. I'd confused being ready to sell with it being the right time to sell.

Exits are the other half of the "you make your money when you buy" saying. You *realize* your money when you sell, and the decision to sell, refinance, or hold deserves the same rigor as the decision to buy.

## The right question: what is my equity earning?

Most owners think about selling in terms of price: "Is this a good price?" The better question is about equity. When you hold a building, your equity is the money you'd have in your pocket if you sold today, after the loan and the costs. That money is *invested* in the building whether you notice or not. The right test for holding is: **does the building earn at least as much on that equity as I could earn by taking it out and putting it somewhere else?**

The calculation is the **marginal return on equity**, the return from holding for one more year:

*(Next year's cash flow + principal paydown + appreciation net of selling costs) / today's equity*

Cash flow is what the building pays you. Principal paydown is equity you build. Appreciation is the expected increase in value (which depends entirely on your assumptions about NOI growth and the cap rate, so be honest). Compare the result to the return you could realistically get elsewhere, after taxes and costs of switching.

Notice that the marginal return typically *falls* over time, for three reasons: the building's growth slows as it matures, your equity grows as the loan amortizes and value rises (so the same dollars of gain are spread over a bigger base), and any value-add upside has already been captured. That is why the question "when should I sell?" often has a good answer: when the marginal return on holding drops below your alternative.

## Taxes and costs change the answer

A sale isn't free. Costs of 2% to 4% of price, plus taxes on the gain (Module 11), come out of your equity. If you sell and pay tax, you redeploy a smaller amount. If you use a 1031 exchange, you redeploy the whole amount but you're constrained by the rules. Both effects favor holding: they raise the bar a new investment has to clear. In Example 1, you'll see that on a taxable sale, a new investment must earn more than 15% on the after-tax proceeds to beat holding.

The opposite pressure is that holding concentrates risk. Over time, a building that has appreciated is a bigger piece of your net worth and a bigger bet on one market. A rule I use: if a single property is more than a quarter of my net worth, I should have a reason, not just inertia.

## Refinancing: taking money out without selling

A **cash-out refinance** replaces your loan with a larger one and hands you the difference. It gives you capital without a sale, so you don't pay sale costs or realize gain (borrowed money isn't taxable income). It sounds like free money, and the part that isn't free is the cost: the new loan is bigger and often at a higher rate than your old one, so your debt service rises and your cash flow falls.

The size of a new loan is set by the smallest of the three tests from Module 5: LTV, DSCR, and debt yield. In a higher-rate environment, DSCR usually binds. Always compute it from the lender's side: new NOI, the quoted rate, and the minimum coverage. Then compute what the extra money costs you: the **drop in cash flow divided by the cash you extracted**. In Example 2, extracting $213,688 costs $15,507 a year in lost cash flow, an effective cost of 7.3%. If you can deploy that cash at a clearly higher return, a refinance helps. If you can't, you've increased your risk to hold money in a savings account.

Also check prepayment on your existing loan. A cash-out refinance often requires paying off a loan with a yield-maintenance or step-down penalty, which can eat the benefit (Module 6). And closing costs of 1% to 2% of the new loan apply every time.

## Hold: the unglamorous choice

Holding is a legitimate strategy, and for some owners the right one for decades. A stabilized, fairly leveraged, well-managed building throws off growing cash flow and builds equity. The reasons to hold: the marginal return exceeds your alternatives; the tax cost of selling is high and you have no replacement; you value the income; or you plan to hold until death for a basis step-up. The reasons to avoid holding out of laziness: concentration risk, large near-term capital needs, a loan maturing into a poor market, or a clear sign the market has peaked.

## Timing and the option to wait

Selling into a weak market has a real cost, and *waiting* has real value if you can afford to. In Example 3, selling at a 7.5% cap rate rather than a 6.5% cap rate costs about $247,000 of value on Maple Court. If you don't have to sell, you can hold, collect cash flow, let NOI grow, and sell when the market improves. But waiting requires something specific: a loan that doesn't mature, reserves that let you ride out surprises, and no partner pressure. The ability to wait is built at the time of borrowing, not at the time of selling. It's one more reason to take the longer loan.

Don't, though, confuse waiting with predicting. I've never known when cap rates would fall. The right approach is to run the numbers for several outcomes (cap rates unchanged, better, worse) and see whether holding still beats selling in the middle one, which is the honest base case.

## Preparing the sale

If you decide to sell, prepare the property the way a buyer's diligence team will examine it. The goal is to avoid a **retrade**: a buyer lowering the price after finding problems during due diligence. The preparation that protects your price:

- **Clean financials**: a T-12 that reconciles to bank statements, a current rent roll, and three years of history. Ironically, the better your books, the less the buyer can renegotiate.
- **Estoppels and leases** organized, and any known issues disclosed upfront.
- **Deferred maintenance addressed** or priced in. A pre-sale inspection can flush out the sewer line or the roof before a buyer does.
- **Operations tuned**: collections current, vacancies leased, below-market rents corrected at renewal where you can, since a buyer will pay a multiple for in-place income.
- **A realistic price.** Overpriced listings sit, and a stale listing signals a problem. A well-priced property with competitive tension often sells above an optimistic ask.
- **A plan for your proceeds**: the 1031 candidate, the debt paydown, or the taxes. Decisions made after the sale are made under time pressure.

A typical sale takes 60 to 120 days from listing to closing for an apartment building, longer for larger or more complex properties. Brokers charge 1% to 3% of price on mid-sized deals, less on larger ones. A broker's opinion of value is a marketing number; get two and ask for the comps behind each.

## Other ways out

Selling for cash isn't the only exit. **Seller financing** (carrying a note for part of the price) can widen your buyer pool and defer taxes through installment treatment, at the cost of taking credit risk on the buyer. A **Delaware statutory trust (DST)** is a pooled ownership structure that can serve as 1031 replacement property for investors who want to stop managing; it brings its own fees and illiquidity, so read the offering carefully. Selling a **partial interest** to a partner or recapitalizing with a new investor can return some capital while keeping control. And **gifting or holding for heirs** takes advantage of the basis step-up at death. None of these is a free lunch; each trades something (control, liquidity, fees, credit risk) for something else. The point is to know the menu before you decide.

## The mistakes I see most

**Selling because you're tired.** Fatigue is a reason to hire a better manager, not necessarily to sell.

**Holding because you're attached.** The building doesn't care about you. Compare the marginal return to the alternatives honestly.

**Ignoring the tax and costs of selling.** They raise the bar for what a new investment must earn.

**A cash-out refinance with no plan for the cash.** Money without a destination tends to be spent.

**Waiting for the perfect price.** Price is a function of the cap rate and NOI; both change. Know the number you'd take, and take it when it comes.

**No plan for the loan maturity.** The most common reason people sell at the wrong time is that their loan forced the date.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Sell when the plan is done.** If you bought for a value-add thesis and it's executed, the remaining return is just ordinary market return, and you should ask whether you want to own that for this building at this basis.

**Don't hold a building you'd not buy today at today's price.** It's a useful test. If, after the improvements and the market moves, you'd not buy this building at its current value with your current equity, why do you own it?

**Sell the weakest building first.** In a portfolio, the building with the lowest marginal return and the highest capital needs is the first candidate to go.

## If you remember nothing else

- The test for holding is the marginal return on your equity, compared with what the equity could earn elsewhere after tax and costs.
- A cash-out refinance isn't free money. Compute its cost as the loss of cash flow divided by the cash extracted.
- The ability to wait is built when you borrow: long loans and reserves make selling optional.

## This week

For a property you own or are modeling, compute the net equity if you sold today (value, minus sale costs, minus loan). Then estimate next year's cash flow, principal paydown, and appreciation, and divide by that equity. Write down what you'd do with the equity if you sold, and the return you'd expect.

# Example 1: Hold or Sell, Maple Court at Year 5

## The setup

You've owned Maple Court for five years. NOI is growing 3% a year, and you expect a 6.5% cap rate to hold. A buyer offers the current market price. You estimate that selling triggers $109,673 of federal tax (Module 11). Your alternative is a new investment you believe can earn 11%, perhaps 14% at best.

## The numbers

Sale today (value $1,854,839 on year-six NOI of $120,565):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sale price | $1,854,839 |
| Sale costs (3%) | -$55,645 |
| Loan balance | -$976,307 |
| **Equity if sold** | **$822,886** |
| Federal tax on the sale | -$109,673 |
| **After-tax proceeds** | **$713,213** |

Holding for one more year:

| Return component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash flow in year 6 ($120,565 - $80,945) | $39,619 |
| Principal paydown | $15,519 |
| Appreciation (value rises to $1,910,484) | $55,645 |
| Extra sale cost on the higher value | -$1,669 |
| **Total return from holding** | **$109,114** |

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Marginal return on equity ($109,114 / $822,886) | 13.3% |
| Marginal return on after-tax proceeds ($109,114 / $713,213) | 15.3% |

What the alternatives would earn:

| Redeploy | At 11% | At 14% |
|---|---:|---:|
| After-tax cash from a taxable sale ($713,213) | $78,453 | $99,850 |
| Full equity via a 1031 exchange ($822,886) | $90,517 | $115,204 |

## What the veteran sees

Holding yields about $109,000 of value for the year. To beat that, a taxable sale must find 15.3% on the reduced cash, and a 1031 exchange 13.3%. At an 11% alternative, selling loses $30,000 to $19,000 a year; even at 14%, a taxable sale barely breaks even ($99,850 against $109,114) and a 1031 wins narrowly ($115,204).

Hold, then, unless the opportunity is both excellent and certain. But there's a caveat: $55,645 of that return is appreciation, and it's an *assumption* (3% growth at an unchanged cap rate). Strip it out and the "hard" return from cash flow and paydown is $55,138, or 6.7% of $822,886. If you don't trust the growth, the marginal return is closer to 7%, and the answer flips for anyone with a good alternative. This is why the honesty of the appreciation estimate is the whole game.

## Change one thing

Suppose the market is frothy and you think the cap rate might widen to 7.0% next year. Next year's value is $124,182 / 0.07 = $1,774,029, less than today's $1,854,839. Return from holding becomes cash flow $39,619 + paydown $15,519 + a value decline of $80,810 (plus a small saving in sale cost) = about -$23,000, a negative marginal return of roughly -$23,000. The answer to "hold or sell" is not a property of the building. It's a property of what you believe about the market. State the belief, then act on it.

# Example 2: The Cash-Out Refinance

## The setup

Instead of selling Maple Court, you consider a cash-out refinance at the end of year 5. Value is $1,854,839 and year-six NOI is $120,565. Your current loan balance is $976,307. The new lender offers 7.0% on a 30-year amortization (constant 7.984%), with the smaller of 70% LTV and a 1.25 DSCR. Closing costs are 1.5% of the new loan.

## The numbers

| | 70% LTV loan | 1.25 DSCR loan (binding) |
|---|---:|---:|
| New loan | $1,298,387 | $1,208,117 |
| Debt service at 7.0% | $103,658 | $96,452 |
| DSCR | 1.16 | 1.25 |
| Closing costs (1.5%) | -$19,476 | -$18,122 |
| Payoff of old loan | -$976,307 | -$976,307 |
| **Net cash out** | **$302,604** | **$213,688** |
| Year-6 cash flow after refi | $16,906 | $24,113 |
| Cash flow before refi | $39,619 | $39,619 |
| Drop in cash flow | -$22,713 | -$15,507 |
| **Effective cost of the cash** | **7.5%** | **7.3%** |

## What the veteran sees

The 70% LTV loan would be the largest, but a lender testing DSCR won't make it: 1.16 is below the usual 1.25 minimum. So the DSCR test binds and the realistic loan is $1,208,117, with $213,688 of cash out. That cash costs 7.3% a year in lost cash flow, roughly your new interest rate. It has no tax, no sale cost, and you keep the building.

Is it worth it? Only if you can deploy the $213,688 at a clearly higher return after risk. At 11%, that's $23,506 a year against a $15,507 cost, a gain of about $8,000 a year. That's real, but modest, and you've added leverage: the DSCR on your original building has gone from 1.49 ($120,565 / $80,945) to 1.25, and you now have less cushion. A cash-out refinance is a trade of safety for liquidity. Make it when you know what the money will do.

## Change one thing

Your old loan has a step-down prepayment penalty of 2% in year 5. On a $976,307 balance, that's $19,526 more. Net cash out falls to $194,162, and the effective cost of the cash rises to $15,507 / $194,162 = 8.0%. The prepayment alone moved the economics. Always get the payoff quote before you run the numbers.

# Example 3: Wait or Sell Into a Weak Market?

## The setup

Cap rates have widened, and a buyer will pay a 7.5% cap on year-six NOI of $120,565. You don't have to sell: your loan runs another five years and you have reserves. You think cap rates will recover to 7.0% in two years. You can reinvest sale proceeds at 11% if you sell now.

## The numbers

| Option | Sale price | Net after costs and loan | Cash flow collected | Total at the end of year 7 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sell now at 7.5% cap | $1,607,527 | $582,994 | $0 | $582,994, which grows to **$718,306** at 11% over two years |
| Wait two years, sell at 7.0% | $1,827,241 | $828,234 | $82,856 | **$911,090** |
| Wait two years, cap stays 7.5% | $1,705,425 | $710,073 | $82,856 | **$792,928** |

Year-eight NOI (the NOI a buyer would underwrite when you sell at the end of year 7) is $127,907. The loan balance after seven years is $944,190.

| Scenario | Wait: total value | Sell now and reinvest | Advantage of waiting |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cap rate recovers to 7.0% | $911,090 | $718,306 | +$192,784 |
| Cap rate stays at 7.5% | $792,928 | $718,306 | +$74,622 |
| Cap rate recovers to 6.5% | $1,047,430 | $718,306 | +$329,124 |
| Cap rate widens to 8.5% | $598,310 | $718,306 | -$119,996 |

## What the veteran sees

Waiting wins in all three of the first scenarios, even the one where the market never improves, because NOI grows 3% a year and the building pays $83,000 in cash while you wait. It loses only if cap rates widen past about 7.85%, roughly a third of a point worse than today. Selling into a weak market locks in the lowest price and gives up two years of income and growth for a reinvestment return that has to overcome that.

The reason waiting is so favorable here is that the owner *can* wait: the loan is long, the cash flow is positive, and there's no partner pressure. Take away those conditions and the answer changes. If the loan matures next year and the refinance gap (Module 12) is $200,000, you aren't waiting, you're being sold. The option to wait is valuable, and it was bought years ago, when you chose your loan.

## Change one thing

Suppose the cap rate goes the wrong way, to 8.5% in two years (the last row of the table). Year-eight NOI of $127,907 / 0.085 = $1,504,787; net after costs and the loan balance is about $515,454, plus cash flow of $82,856: about $598,310, which is $119,996 below the $718,306 you could have had by selling now and reinvesting. Waiting isn't free; it is a bet that cap rates won't widen by more than about 0.35 points beyond today's 7.5%. Decide how much market risk you're willing to take for the possible $75,000 to $329,000 upside, and look at the downside as carefully as the upside.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

You own a stabilized building with $822,886 of equity. Holding for one more year is projected to produce $109,114 from cash flow, principal paydown, and appreciation. What is the most useful way to decide whether to sell?

- [ ] Sell whenever the price is above what you paid, since any gain over your cost is a good result :: A gain over your purchase price says nothing about whether the equity is earning enough where it sits. Compare the marginal return with alternatives.
- [x] Compare the 13.3% marginal return with your alternatives. :: Equity in the property is capital you've chosen to keep invested. Selling only makes sense if redeployment, after tax and costs, earns more.
- [ ] Hold until the loan is fully paid off, because debt-free ownership is the goal of every investor :: The loan balance is not a decision rule. The relevant question is what your equity earns, not whether the debt is gone.
- [ ] Sell as soon as the business plan is complete, whatever the marginal return on the equity turns out to be :: A finished plan is a reason to look at the question, but the decision depends on the marginal return and the alternatives.

> Your equity is always invested. The question is whether it's invested in the best place you have.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** A taxable sale of your building would net $713,213 after tax. Holding is projected to return $109,114 next year, of which $55,645 is expected appreciation. A new investment you've found offers an expected 12% on whatever you put in. You are not confident in the 3% annual growth assumption.

What is the soundest conclusion?

- [ ] Sell, because the 12% on offer beats the 13.3% return from holding the building for another year :: 12% does not beat 13.3%. And the correct comparison against after-tax cash would be 15.3% for holding, which is even higher.
- [ ] Hold, because the holding return is larger than the alternative no matter which assumptions you use :: It's larger only if the appreciation assumption is right. Without the $55,645, the hard return is about 6.7% of equity.
- [x] It hinges on the appreciation assumption, so test that first. :: With growth, holding wins; without it, a 12% alternative would. Understanding what you believe about growth and the cap rate is the real decision.
- [ ] Sell, because the cash flow and the principal paydown alone clearly fall short of the 12% on offer :: That's true of the hard return, but ignoring growth entirely isn't the base case. You need to decide how much of the appreciation you believe.

> A hold-or-sell decision is a decision about what you believe, so write the belief down.

## Q3 | scenario

**Scenario.** A lender offers a cash-out refinance on a building worth $1,854,839 with a 70% LTV ($1,298,387) at 7.0%. Year-six NOI is $120,565 and your existing loan balance is $976,307. The lender's minimum DSCR is 1.25, and the 70% LTV loan would have a DSCR of 1.16.

What loan can you actually get, and what does it cost you?

- [ ] $1,298,387, because the lender's LTV limit is the main test and that is the largest loan allowed :: LTV is one of several tests. A loan with a DSCR of 1.16 fails the lender's 1.25 minimum, so it would be cut back.
- [x] About $1,208,000, limited by DSCR, at roughly 7.3% a year. :: The DSCR test binds. The cash costs $15,507 a year in lost cash flow, which is 7.3% of the $213,688 extracted.
- [ ] About $976,000, since a refinancing cannot increase the size of the loan beyond its current balance :: A cash-out refinance exists precisely to increase the loan when value and income support it.
- [ ] The cash is free, because borrowed money is not taxable income and the loan carries no real cost :: It isn't taxed, but it isn't free. You pay interest, and your cash flow falls by about 7.3% of the cash.

> The cheapest-looking money has a price: the lost cash flow. Divide it by the cash and compare it to what you will do with it.

## Q4 | multiple_choice

You decide to sell a 40-unit building. Which preparation most directly protects your price from a buyer's retrade during due diligence?

- [ ] Choosing the broker who gives the highest asking-price opinion, to set a strong starting point :: An inflated opinion of value attracts a listing but not a sale; it can lead to a stale listing and, later, a larger retrade.
- [ ] Cutting the asking price well below market value so that the sale closes as quickly as possible :: This protects against delay but gives away value. A retrade is about what a buyer finds, not about the asking price.
- [x] Financials that reconcile to the bank, and a pre-sale inspection. :: Buyers cut price when they find surprises. Reconciled books and known issues, fixed or disclosed, leave them little to find.
- [ ] Keeping the rent roll and the leases confidential until the buyer is firmly under contract :: Withholding documents invites suspicion and delays. Buyers price uncertainty by lowering their offers.

> A buyer retrades what they discover. Discover it first.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** Cap rates have widened to 7.5%. A buyer offers $1,607,527 for your building. You have a long-term loan, reserves, and no pressure to sell. You expect cap rates to recover to 7.0% in two years, and NOI is growing 3% a year. The risk is that cap rates widen further to 8.5%.

What should guide your decision?

- [ ] Sell now, since no one can predict cap rates and waiting is simply a form of gambling :: Not predicting is not the same as having no basis. You can weigh the scenarios and see that waiting wins unless cap rates widen by more than about a third of a point.
- [ ] Wait, because cap rates always return to their previous level given enough time and patience :: They don't always recover, and in the 8.5% scenario waiting loses against selling now and reinvesting.
- [x] Compare outcomes across cap-rate scenarios, and note you can wait. :: Waiting wins in the 7.5%, 7.0%, and 6.5% scenarios and loses at 8.5%, with break-even near a 7.85% cap rate. Your ability to hold, set up by your loan and reserves, is what makes waiting an option.
- [ ] Ask the buyer to raise the price by the full amount of the cap-rate widening since your purchase :: A buyer pricing at 7.5% has no reason to pay a 6.5% price. The negotiation does not change the market.

> The option to wait has value only if you can afford to exercise it. Build that ability before you need it.
