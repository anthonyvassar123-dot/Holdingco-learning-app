# Lesson

The best money I ever saved came from a $450 bank statement review. A seller's broker had packaged a 30-unit building with a clean T-12 and a rent roll that matched it to the dollar. The numbers looked great. When I asked for the twelve months of bank statements, it took the seller two weeks and a lot of explanations to produce them. Deposits ran 6% below reported income. The gap wasn't theft; it was a pile of ordinary things: rent credits handed out to keep tenants, bad debts booked as collected, and one month of rent deposited in January that had been counted in December. But 6% of income at that building's cap rate was a $350,000 pricing error. I renegotiated, and the seller took it. The buyer who came before me, I later learned, hadn't asked.

Due diligence is a process for finding out whether the building you think you're buying is the building you're actually buying. It's also a process with a clock and a price tag, which is why the order you do things in matters as much as what you do.

## The contract clock

Once you sign a purchase agreement, you typically have a **due diligence period**, commonly 30 to 45 days for apartments and 45 to 60 for commercial. During it, your earnest money deposit is usually refundable. When it expires, the deposit goes "hard" (non-refundable) unless you've terminated. That date is the most important date in the deal. Everything below has to be finished, or extended in writing, before it.

Money goes out the door during diligence whether or not you close. In the mid-2020s, a $4 to $6 million apartment purchase might run: building inspection $3,000 to $8,000; Phase I environmental $2,500 to $4,500; property condition assessment $2,500 to $6,000; survey $4,000 to $8,000; appraisal $4,000 to $7,000; legal $15,000 to $30,000; lender third-party reports and fees on top. Total at-risk cost: $40,000 to $70,000. You don't want to spend it all on a deal you could have killed on day three for free.

## Sequence: cheap tests first

The principle is simple: do the checks that cost the least and kill the most deals first. In my experience that order is:

1. **Financial verification (days 1 to 7).** Rent roll, T-12, twelve months of bank statements, tax returns, the lease for every unit or a sample, a list of all service contracts, and utility bills. Cost: your time. Kills: more deals than anything else.
2. **Leases and estoppels (days 3 to 15).** Read the leases (all of them for commercial), and send estoppel requests. Cost: small legal time.
3. **Title, survey, and zoning (days 5 to 20).** Order the title commitment and survey early; they take the longest. Look for easements, encroachments, liens, a zoning classification that doesn't match the use. Cost: moderate.
4. **Insurance and tax quotes (days 5 to 15).** Get a real insurance quote, and find out how taxes are reassessed. These flow straight into NOI.
5. **Physical inspections (days 10 to 25).** Building systems, roof, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, sewer scope, parking, and a unit-level sample. Cost: significant, so do it after the financials hold up.
6. **Environmental (days 10 to 30).** A Phase I report on any commercial property or any older building near a past industrial or dry-cleaning use. If it identifies a "recognized environmental condition," you may need a Phase II with soil sampling, which costs more and takes longer.
7. **Market and operations (ongoing).** Check comps again, call leasing offices, and speak with the property manager.

## The three-way reconciliation

The most valuable financial test is to make three documents agree with each other:

- The **rent roll** says who lives where and what they pay.
- The **T-12** says what the seller claims they collected.
- The **bank statements** say what actually arrived.

Annualize the rent roll for occupied units and compare it to the T-12 income. Then compare T-12 income to total deposits. Explain every gap. Legitimate gaps exist: vacancy turnover during the year, rent increases mid-year, prepaid rents, security deposits that must be excluded. What you're looking for is the *unexplained* remainder. In Example 1, a 6.2% gap between reported income and deposits resolves into bad debt, concessions, timing, and $2,200 nobody can explain.

Also test the expenses: pick the three biggest lines and match them to invoices or statements. Test the property tax line to the actual tax bill and the insurance line to the policy declaration page. One caution I give everyone: *a seller who can't or won't produce bank statements is telling you something.*

## Leases and estoppels

For multifamily, read a sample of leases (I read 20%) and check them against the rent roll for rent amount, term, deposit, and special terms. For commercial, read every lease, line by line. You're looking for options (renewal, termination, expansion, purchase), exclusive-use clauses, expense caps, free rent, tenant improvement obligations, and anything that doesn't match the summary.

An **estoppel certificate** is a signed statement from the tenant confirming the facts of the lease: the rent, the term, the deposit, that there are no defaults, no side agreements, no unpaid concessions. It's the only evidence that doesn't come from the seller. For commercial deals, get them from every tenant, or from all tenants representing at least 80% of the rent. They have a habit of revealing what the rent roll doesn't, as you'll see in Example 3. Lenders will require them too.

## The physical inspection: what to look for and how to price it

A building inspection is not a pass/fail. It's a list of problems, each of which becomes either a price reduction, a seller credit, a closing condition, or a line in your reserve plan. Sort every finding into three bins:

- **Life-safety and immediate** (fix before or at closing): fire alarms, electrical hazards, collapsed sewer lines, mold, structural movement.
- **Near-term capital** (due within one to three years): roofs near end of life, water heaters, boilers, parking lots, windows.
- **Long-term and cosmetic** (budget for it): paint, flooring, landscaping.

For older buildings, always scope the sewer line with a camera ($300 to $600). Failed laterals are among the most common unpleasant surprises in older buildings, and a $38,500 repair is cheap compared with discovering it after closing. Ask about the water heater age, the roof type and remaining life, and electrical panels with known problems. A roof typically costs $5 to $12 per square foot to replace on a flat commercial or multifamily building.

Then compare the total of near-term items to what your underwriting reserves cover. If you reserved $250 a unit, a 36-unit building has $9,000 a year. A $337,300 list of needs is not covered by $27,000 of reserves over three years. That's the gap between a model and a building.

## Title, survey, zoning

The title commitment lists who owns the property, any liens, and any easements. The survey shows where the building sits relative to the lot lines and easements. Zoning determines what can be built and used. Common problems: a "legal nonconforming" use that can't be rebuilt after a fire; parking that doesn't meet code; an easement across the parking lot; a lien from an unpaid contractor. Your attorney handles the mechanics, but ask directly: if this building burns down, can it be rebuilt as it stands?

## A diligence calendar that works

Here's the rough rhythm I run on a 45-day period for a mid-sized apartment building. **Days 1 to 3:** send the full document request to the seller's broker, order the title commitment and survey, and get an insurance quote. **Days 4 to 10:** receive documents, reconcile the three-way income test, and read leases. If anything is off by more than 3%, call the broker before spending another dollar. **Days 10 to 20:** schedule the inspections for the same two days so you can walk the property once with everyone, and send estoppels. **Days 20 to 30:** review the reports, price the findings, and write one consolidated renegotiation letter, not a drip of requests. **Days 30 to 40:** finalize the loan terms and lock the rate. **Days 40 to 45:** decide. Either you close on the terms, you extend in writing, or you terminate before the deposit goes hard.

## Questions that open people up

Some of the best diligence is conversation. I ask the on-site manager: which units do you dread turning, and why? What gets called in at 2 a.m.? Which tenants have been here longest? I ask the maintenance person what he'd fix first if it were his money. I ask neighbors what the building was like three years ago. And I ask the seller one simple question: *"If you were staying, what would you do with the property in the next three years?"* The answer tells you what he knows about its condition, and whether he has been starving it of capital. If he says "nothing, it's fine," look harder. If he says "replace the roof and the boilers," you've just been handed a negotiating list.

## The mistakes I see most

**Doing the expensive tests first.** Spending $8,000 on an inspection before verifying that the income exists is the most common waste.

**Trusting summaries.** A rent roll is a summary. A T-12 is a summary. Leases and bank statements are the source documents.

**Letting the clock run.** The expiration of diligence is a bright line. If something isn't resolved, extend in writing before the date, not after.

**Treating the inspector as the whole process.** An inspector looks at the building. Nobody looks at the leases, the taxes, the insurance, and the market unless you do.

**Not asking the neighbors.** Walk the property at night and on a weekend. Talk to the people next door. Ask a tenant, with the manager out of earshot, what the place is like.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Price adjustment versus walking away.** Some findings are price items: a roof, a parking lot, a deferred maintenance list. They have a dollar number. Others are deal-killers: undisclosed contamination, a title defect you can't cure, fraud in the financials, a building structurally compromised. The test: *can I put a number on it, and is the seller willing to pay it?* If you can't price a problem, you shouldn't own it.

**How hard to press.** If diligence turns up $100,000 of surprises, the seller will often give most of it back; if you ask for every penny and a list of small things, you can lose the deal over $4,000. I ask for the large, documentable items and let the small ones go. People are more willing to concede a fair, short list than a long, aggressive one.

**When the seller's story changes.** If an explanation for a gap changes the second time you ask, or two documents disagree and no one can reconcile them, I stop. The chance that the one problem I found is the only problem is low.

## If you remember nothing else

- Do the cheap tests first: the financial reconciliation kills more bad deals than any inspection.
- Reconcile rent roll, T-12, and bank deposits. Explain every gap; the unexplained remainder is a red flag.
- Turn every finding into a number: a price cut, a credit, a reserve, or a walk.

# Example 1: The 6% Gap

## The setup

You're buying Hollis Park, a 30-unit building priced on a 6.75% cap rate. You've asked for the rent roll, the T-12, and twelve months of bank statements.

## The numbers

| Source | Annual income |
|---|---:|
| Rent roll, occupied units ($36,400 × 12) | $436,800 |
| T-12 reported rental income | $428,000 |
| Bank deposits, twelve months | $401,500 |

T-12 versus rent roll: -2.0% (explained by two units turning over during the year). T-12 versus deposits: **-$26,500, or -6.2%**.

| Reconciling the $26,500 gap | Amount | Recurring? |
|---|---:|---|
| Bad debt written off after being booked as income | $11,800 | Yes |
| Rent credits and concessions booked as full income | $9,600 | Yes |
| December rent deposited in January | $2,900 | No, timing |
| Unexplained | $2,200 | Treat as yes until explained |
| **Total** | **$26,500** | |

Recurring items to remove from NOI: $11,800 + $9,600 + $2,200 = $23,600. At a 6.75% cap rate: $23,600 / 0.0675 = **$349,630** of value.

## What the veteran sees

Three of the four reasons are ordinary. None is fraud; each is a way a bookkeeper smooths a story. The point is that the T-12 was income earned, and you pay for income collected. The $349,630 is the difference between the two, and it's the price of reading the bank statements.

The $2,200 matters more than its size: it's the part nobody can explain. A small unexplained number in a clean set of books is noise; in a set of books that has already surprised you, it's a place to dig.

## Change one thing

Say the seller convinces you the bad debt was a one-off, tied to a single tenant who has since been evicted. Then the recurring leak is $9,600 + $2,200 = $11,800, and the value gap drops to $174,815. That is a legitimate argument, but it's yours to verify: check the collections history unit by unit, and ask how many units in the last twelve months ended with unpaid balances. Don't accept a one-off from a seller who benefits from it being one.

# Example 2: From Inspection Report to Price

## The setup

Willow Court is a 36-unit building under contract at $4,800,000. Your inspections produce a list of findings. Your underwriting reserves are $250 per unit per year.

## The numbers

| Finding | Cost | Timing |
|---|---:|---|
| Sewer lateral collapse (60 ft) | $38,500 | Immediate |
| Parking lot: patch, seal, restripe | $46,000 | Immediate |
| Smoke detectors and GFCI outlets | $14,000 | Immediate (life-safety) |
| **Immediate subtotal** | **$98,500** | |
| Flat roof replacement (28,000 sf at $7.50) | $210,000 | Years 1-3 |
| Water heaters at end of life (12 × $2,400) | $28,800 | Years 1-3 |
| **Near-term subtotal** | **$238,800** | |
| **Total identified needs** | **$337,300** | $9,369 per unit |

Reserves in your model: $250 × 36 units × 3 years = **$27,000**, which covers 8% of the identified needs.

## What the veteran sees

Your model said the building needed $9,000 a year. The inspection says it needs more than $110,000 a year over three years. Neither is wrong; they are answering different questions. The reserve is a long-run average. The inspection is a snapshot of deferred maintenance that the seller didn't fund.

How do you get it back? The immediate items are life-safety and unambiguous. Ask for a dollar-for-dollar price reduction or credit: $98,500. The roof and water heaters are harder: a roof near the end of its life has value to the seller, since they got the use of it. A fair negotiating position is to ask for half the roof ($105,000), since you'll be paying for the second half of its life cycle. An ask of $98,500 + $105,000 = $203,500, or 4.2% of the price, is substantial but defensible with documents. Also adjust your first-three-year reserve plan to include the rest.

## Change one thing

The seller offers to repair the sewer and parking lot before closing instead of giving a credit. Think twice. A seller doing the work is incentivized to do it cheaply, to your standards or not. If you accept, require a licensed contractor, permits where required, a warranty, and a re-inspection by your inspector before closing. In most cases, I prefer cash at closing, because it means I control the work and the quality.

# Example 3: What the Estoppels Said

## The setup

Marlow Plaza is a 20,000-square-foot retail building with 12 tenants and an annual rent roll of $510,000 (an average of $25.50 per square foot). You've asked every tenant for an estoppel. Nine come back within two weeks. Three of them disagree with the rent roll.

## The numbers

| Tenant | Rent roll says | Estoppel says | Annual impact |
|---|---|---|---:|
| A (4,000 sf) | $26.00 per sf | $24.00 per sf | -$8,000 |
| B (6,500 a month) | No concession | 6 months of free rent still owed to the tenant | -$39,000 (one-time) |
| C (3,000 sf) | $26.00 per sf, 5-year term remaining | Renewal option at a fixed $20.00 per sf | -$18,000 from the renewal year |

Recurring annual reduction: $8,000 + $18,000 = **$26,000**. At a 7.0% cap rate: $26,000 / 0.07 = $371,429. Plus the one-time $39,000 free rent obligation: **$410,429** in total.

## What the veteran sees

None of this is visible in the rent roll. Tenant A's rent was reduced at some point and nobody updated the schedule. Tenant B was given free rent that the seller kept off the books, probably to keep the rent roll high. Tenant C holds an option that will cap your rent at a number below market. The first is an error, the second is a hidden liability, and the third is a structural limit on the building's value. Three tenants out of nine is a 33% error rate, and three unseen tenants might have the same issue.

Estoppels also test the seller. If a seller explains the mismatch with plausible detail ("A negotiated a rent cut in March that we hadn't updated"), it's an honest mistake. If the explanation is vague, defensive, or contradicts the other documents, press for more evidence.

## Change one thing

The three tenants who haven't returned estoppels are 1,800, 2,200, and 2,000 square feet, together 30% of the building. Under most purchase agreements, you can require them as a condition of closing. If you extend diligence by two weeks and they confirm the rent roll, you've bought certainty. If they don't answer, you don't know what's there. Treat silence from a tenant as a risk you must price, not as a yes.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

You have 30 days of due diligence and a limited budget. Why is it usually best to verify rent, income, and bank deposits before paying for a building inspection?

- [ ] Because financial documents are far easier to read and understand than inspection reports are :: Ease of reading isn't the reason. The sequencing is about cost and information: some tests are cheap and decisive, others are expensive and often unnecessary.
- [x] They're cheap and can kill a deal before big spending. :: Income that doesn't reconcile ends many deals at almost no cost. If the numbers fail, the inspection money is never spent.
- [ ] Because lenders always require the financial verification to be complete before any other test :: Lenders do require it, but the reason to sequence this way is your own risk and cost. You would do it first even without a lender.
- [ ] Because physical inspections can only be performed by the buyer after the closing has occurred :: Inspections happen during diligence, before your deposit goes hard. The question is when within that window you spend the money.

> Spend small money first to find out whether it's worth spending big money.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** A building's T-12 reports $428,000 of income, but twelve months of bank deposits total $401,500. The seller explains that $11,800 was bad debt, $9,600 was rent credits, $2,900 was a December rent deposited in January, and $2,200 he can't explain. You're valuing at a 6.75% cap rate.

What is the best way to treat the gap in your underwriting?

- [ ] Ignore it, since the T-12 is the seller's official statement of income and the deal is priced on it :: The T-12 is a claim. Bank deposits are evidence, and the difference is $26,500 of income that never arrived.
- [ ] Deduct the full $26,500 from NOI as a recurring loss and move on to the next item :: The December timing item ($2,900) is one-time and shouldn't be capitalized. Deducting all of it overstates the recurring loss.
- [x] Remove the recurring items, and treat the $2,200 as recurring until explained. :: That's $23,600 a year of income you won't collect, worth about $349,630 at a 6.75% cap. The timing item is not recurring.
- [ ] Accept the seller's explanation in full and keep the original NOI figure in your underwriting :: The explanation describes real losses of collected income. Bad debt and rent credits recur, so capitalizing the original NOI overpays.

> You pay for income collected, not income reported. Separate the recurring leaks from the one-time timing items.

## Q3 | scenario

**Scenario.** Your inspection of a 36-unit building identifies $98,500 of immediate repairs (sewer, parking, life-safety) and $238,800 of near-term items (a roof and water heaters), against a purchase price of $4,800,000. Your model reserves $27,000 for the next three years. The seller says the building is "sold as is."

Which approach to the negotiation is most sound?

- [ ] Ask for the full $337,300 off the price, since all of it is deferred maintenance the seller skipped :: An aggressive all-in ask on items that include long-life components like the roof invites a standoff over a deal that's otherwise good.
- [ ] Accept the building as-is and rely on the $27,000 reserve in your model to cover whatever comes up :: The reserve covers only 8% of what's identified. Taking the building as-is means paying out of pocket for what the seller deferred.
- [x] Get a $98,500 credit and a share of the roof, and raise reserves. :: Immediate and life-safety items have a clear cost and should come back to you. A fair share of the roof is defensible, and the remaining needs must be in your plan.
- [ ] Let the seller do all the repairs before closing without any permits or verification of the work :: A seller doing the work has an incentive to do it cheaply. Without permits, a warranty, and your inspector's re-check, you inherit the risk.

> Every finding becomes a number: a credit, a reserve, or a walk. "As is" starts the negotiation, not ends it.

## Q4 | multiple_choice

What is the main value of getting estoppel certificates from commercial tenants?

- [ ] They provide the seller with legal protection from claims by the buyer after the closing is complete :: Estoppels protect the buyer and lender by confirming facts, not the seller's post-closing position.
- [x] They confirm lease terms directly with the tenant. :: A signed statement of rent, term, deposit, concessions, and defaults is the only evidence that doesn't come from the seller, so it catches errors and hidden side deals.
- [ ] They replace the need to read the leases, since they summarize every term that matters :: Estoppels summarize facts but leases contain options, exclusives, and caps that can matter greatly. You need both.
- [ ] They guarantee the tenant will renew at the end of the lease term at the stated option rent :: An estoppel certifies current facts. It makes no promise about future behavior.

> A rent roll is what the seller says. An estoppel is what the tenant says. Trust the one that has nothing to gain.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** A Phase I environmental report on a small commercial building you've put under contract identifies a recognized environmental condition: a dry-cleaning business operated on the site in the 1980s. The Phase I recommends a Phase II (soil and groundwater sampling). Your diligence period expires in 12 days. The sampling will take about three weeks.

What should you do?

- [ ] Waive the Phase II and close on time, since the dry cleaner closed decades ago and is long gone :: Contamination from dry-cleaning solvents can persist for decades. Under most environmental laws, the current owner can be liable regardless of who caused it.
- [x] Extend in writing, finish the Phase II, and be ready to walk. :: You can't price contamination you haven't measured, and ownership can bring cleanup liability. If sampling confirms contamination, walking away is usually the right call.
- [ ] Ask for a modest price reduction to cover the risk and close on the original schedule :: An unquantified environmental risk can run to six or seven figures. A small discount doesn't match a risk you haven't measured.
- [ ] Terminate immediately, because any environmental finding at all is automatically a deal-killer :: A recognized condition isn't a confirmed problem. The Phase II is what tells you whether it's a price item or a deal-killer.

> If you can't put a number on a problem, you shouldn't own it. Measure first, then decide.
