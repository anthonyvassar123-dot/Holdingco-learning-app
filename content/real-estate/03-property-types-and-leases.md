# Lesson

A man I'll call Hal bought a single-tenant building leased to a national drugstore chain, with fifteen years on the lease. He paid a 6.25% cap rate. His broker called it "a bond with a roof," and for nine years it behaved like one: the rent hit his account on the first of every month, no calls, no repairs. In year ten the chain closed that store and moved a block away to a new building with a drive-through. They didn't break the lease. They just stopped operating, locked the door, and kept paying rent for the remaining five years, because the lease allowed it. Hal had a "dark" building with a paying tenant. He couldn't re-let it, couldn't sell it for anything close to what he paid, and knew that the day the lease ended, he'd own a 9,000-square-foot drugstore in a spot the drugstore had just abandoned.

Hal had read the rent clause and the term. He had never read the clause about the right to go dark. That's the kind of thing this module is about: what you buy isn't just a building or a rent number; it's a contract, and every contract has a day it ends.

## Property types are really income types

Property types matter less for what they look like than for how their income fails. Think of each as a different kind of cash flow with a different kind of risk.

**Multifamily (apartments).** Short leases, usually 12 months. Many tenants, so no one tenant matters. Rents reset to the market every year, so you ride inflation up and you ride recessions down. Risk is *granular and continuous*: it shows up as vacancy and turnover cost, rarely as a cliff. Lenders love it, which is why it carries the best financing.

**Single-tenant net lease.** One tenant, long lease (often 10 to 20 years), tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Looks like a bond. Risk is *binary and delayed*: if the tenant is solid, nothing happens for years; if they leave, you lose 100% of the income at once, with a building that may be specialized.

**Multi-tenant retail (strip centers, shopping centers).** A handful to dozens of tenants, mixed lease lengths, usually triple net. The anchor tenant (grocery, drugstore) draws traffic for everyone, and smaller tenants' leases often depend on the anchor staying. Risk is *structural*: lose the anchor and the whole center may reprice.

**Office.** Longer leases than apartments (3 to 10 years), heavy tenant improvement costs at every renewal, and a market that has changed structurally. Remote work shrank demand for older space in the 2020s while rewarding the newest, best-located buildings. Risk is *obsolescence*: you can do everything right and still own space the market doesn't want.

**Industrial and warehouse.** Simple buildings, strong demand for the last decade, low capital spend. But as rents rose, a lot of supply arrived. Risk is *timing and location*: the building is only as good as its access to highways, ports, and labor.

**Self-storage, mobile home parks, and others.** Smaller niches with their own economics: very short leases (storage is month-to-month), low operating cost, and institutional buyers who've pushed cap rates down. The principle is the same: ask how the income fails.

## Lease structures: who pays for what

The lease decides who bears the costs of running the building. It's the single most important contract in commercial real estate, and the three structures are worth burning in.

**Gross lease (full-service).** The tenant pays one rent, and the landlord pays all operating expenses. Simple for the tenant. Dangerous for the landlord when expenses grow, because the rent is fixed.

**Modified gross.** The tenant pays one rent, but pays *increases* in expenses above a "base year" (an expense stop). The landlord is protected from inflation after year one.

**Triple net (NNN).** The tenant pays base rent *plus* its share of property taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance. The landlord's income is the base rent; expenses pass through. An **absolute net** (or "bondable") lease goes further: the tenant also pays for structural repairs and the roof. These are the closest thing in real estate to a bond.

**Other terms worth knowing.** *Percentage rent* gives the landlord a share of a retailer's sales above a threshold. *Escalations* are rent bumps, either fixed (say 2% a year or 10% every five years) or tied to CPI. *Renewal options* give the tenant the right, not the obligation, to extend at a stated rent or a market rent. *Tenant improvements (TI)* are the cash the landlord pays to build out the space for a new tenant, and *leasing commissions (LC)* are the broker's fee, often 4% to 6% of the aggregate rent. *Free rent* is a concession that reduces the real rent in early months.

## Credit is the engine of the long lease

A long lease is only as good as the tenant behind it. The same 15-year lease means very different things depending on who signs:

- **Investment-grade corporate tenant:** the lease is nearly a bond, and buyers pay accordingly. Cap rates are low (in the mid-2020s, roughly 5.5% to 6.5% for strong credit with long terms).
- **Regional chain or franchisee:** the guarantee matters. Is it a corporate guarantee from the parent, or an LLC with one store? The latter is an unsecured promise from a small business.
- **Local small business:** shorter lease, personal guarantee if you're lucky, and your real security is the building itself.

Ask for the tenant's financials. Ask whether the lease has a **right to go dark** (can they stop operating and still pay rent?), a **termination option** (can they leave early for a fee?), and **co-tenancy clauses** (does their rent drop if an anchor leaves?). Any of these can quietly turn a 15-year lease into a 3-year one.

## The day after the lease ends

This is the part I want you to take seriously. When you buy a building with a lease of, say, seven years remaining, the headline cap rate assumes the rent continues. In reality there are two futures:

1. **The tenant renews.** You pay modest leasing costs and maybe a small tenant improvement allowance. The rent resets to the market, which might be higher or lower than today's.
2. **The tenant leaves.** You carry the empty building (taxes, insurance, utilities), pay a broker, fund a new build-out, and offer free rent to the next tenant. For a retail or office space, that can run $40 to $80 a square foot, plus a year or more of lost rent.

The probability-weighted outcome is what you actually own. In Example 1 you'll see how a 6.25% cap rate on a seven-year-remaining lease becomes a 3.5% unlevered return once you price in rollover. That isn't a reason to avoid net lease. It's the reason to pay the right price for it, and to ask harder questions about the building's alternative uses.

## Weighted-average lease term and the shape of the rollover

Two numbers summarize a multi-tenant rent roll. **WALT**, the weighted-average lease term, tells you how long the average dollar of rent is contracted for. But averages hide shape, so you also need the **rollover schedule**: what percentage of rent expires in each of the next five years. A WALT of 5.4 years sounds comfortable; but if a quarter of the rent expires in the next three years and 76% comes from two tenants, you own a very different risk than the average implies.

## What re-leasing costs, in rough numbers

Every property type has a different price for turning one tenant into the next. These are ranges for the mid-2020s, vary hugely by market and condition, and are only here so you have a sense of scale before you ask a local broker for the real figures:

- **Apartments:** $2,500 to $4,500 to turn a unit (paint, flooring, cleaning, lost rent for 10 to 18 days). Per-unit and quick.
- **Industrial:** $5 to $20 per square foot in tenant improvements for a second-generation space, with 3 to 9 months of downtime.
- **Retail inline space:** $20 to $60 per square foot of TI, a commission of 4% to 6% of the total lease rent, and 6 to 12 months to find and sign someone.
- **Office:** $50 to $120 per square foot of TI for a new tenant in a competitive building, plus free rent (a month or more per year of lease term) and commissions, with 9 to 18 months of downtime.

Multiply those by the square footage on a rent roll and the rollover schedule stops being an abstraction. A 20,000-square-foot office floor that leaves can take $1.2 million and a year and a half to refill.

## The mistakes I see most

**Reading the rent and not the lease.** Rent is the headline. The right to go dark, the termination option, the co-tenancy clause, the assignment rights, and the exclusive-use clauses are the details that decide how the income behaves in a bad year.

**Treating the cap rate as the return.** On a single-tenant building with a finite lease, the going-in cap rate is only the return if the tenant renews at today's rent forever. It never is.

**Ignoring expense exposure in gross leases.** A fixed rent in an inflationary period is a slow-motion pay cut. Insurance in some coastal and wildfire-exposed markets has risen 30% to 100% in a year. If your lease has no expense stop, that comes straight out of your NOI.

**Falling for credit by logo.** The tenant's name on the sign isn't necessarily the entity on the lease. Ask who signed, whether there's a parent guarantee, and what the guarantee actually covers.

**Not asking what the building could be if the tenant left.** A bank branch, a drive-through restaurant, a car wash: ask the question "who else would pay rent for this?" before you buy.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Pay for the second tenant, not the first.** The first tenant's lease gets you through a bit of the hold period. What you own for decades is the next tenant's willingness to pay. Underwrite the location and the building as if the first tenant leaves.

**Multifamily versus net lease for a small buyer.** If you have limited reserves, the granular risk of apartments (one unit at a time) is usually easier to survive than the binary risk of a single-tenant property where 100% of income can vanish with one decision in another city. Net lease rewards the buyer who can absorb a year or two of zero income.

**Don't buy the lease when you can't buy the dirt.** Where the land is scarce and valuable, a below-market lease can still be a good deal, since the underlying real estate has value. Where the land is cheap and the building is special-purpose, you're relying on the tenant alone.

## If you remember nothing else

- Every property type is a way the income can fail. Name it before you buy.
- Know who pays for expense inflation. A gross lease without an expense stop puts it on you.
- The headline cap rate assumes the tenant renews at today's rent. Price what happens the day after the lease ends.

# Example 1: The 6.25% Cap Rate That Isn't

## The setup

You're offered a 9,500-square-foot single-tenant building for $2,400,000. The tenant pays $150,000 of net rent a year ($15.79 per square foot, absolute net) with seven years remaining. Cap rate: $150,000 / $2,400,000 = 6.25%. Market rent for this space is $14.75 per square foot, so the tenant is paying about 6.6% above market. You assume flat rent through the term to keep the math clean (most leases have bumps, and bumps tend to widen the gap to market, not close it).

## The numbers

At lease expiry, the market rent NOI is 9,500 × $14.75 = **$140,125**.

| Scenario at expiry (year 7) | Tenant leaves | Tenant renews |
|---|---:|---:|
| Stabilized value on market NOI ($140,125) | $2,001,786 (7.0% cap) | $2,075,926 (6.75% cap) |
| Tenant improvements ($30/sf leaves; $5/sf renews) | -$285,000 | -$47,500 |
| Leasing commissions (6% × 5 years / 3% × 5 years) | -$42,038 | -$21,019 |
| Carrying cost during a year of vacancy ($3.10/sf) | -$29,450 | $0 |
| **Value at expiry, net of costs** | **$1,645,298** | **$2,007,407** |

Unlevered IRR of buying at $2,400,000, collecting $150,000 a year for seven years, and then selling at those values:

| Outcome | Unlevered IRR |
|---|---:|
| Tenant leaves | 2.0% |
| Tenant renews | 4.2% |
| 65% chance of renewal (weighted value $1,880,669) | 3.5% |

## What the veteran sees

The headline says 6.25%. The probability-weighted return is about 3.5%. The gap comes from three places: the rent is above market, so renewal means a rent cut; the building is worth less on rollover day than you paid for it; and rollover costs are real money. A seven-year lease at 6.25% is a bond that matures in a worse building than you bought.

That doesn't make it a bad deal at the right price. What price gets you a 6.5% return on that weighted view? Solving, the price is about **$2,032,900**, roughly 15% below the ask. If the seller won't move, your return comes from somewhere else, such as a lower interest rate or the real estate's upside, and you should name where.

## Change one thing

Say the tenant is stronger than assumed, and renewal odds are 80%. The weighted value at expiry is $1,934,985 and the unlevered IRR improves to 3.8%. Credit helps, but only by about three tenths of a point. The price paid matters far more than the odds you assign. That's why veterans haggle on price harder than they agonize about tenant probability.

# Example 2: Who Pays When Costs Rise

## The setup

You're comparing three lease structures for a 5,000-square-foot office suite. The tenant will pay roughly the same total either way, about $28 a square foot, but who bears expense growth is different. Operating expenses are $9.50 per square foot in year one and grow 4% a year.

- **Full-service gross, no stop:** tenant pays $28 flat. Landlord pays all expenses.
- **Modified gross with a base-year stop:** tenant pays $28 plus any expense increase over year one's $9.50.
- **NNN:** tenant pays $19 base rent, plus all expenses. Landlord nets $19.

## The numbers

Landlord's net income per square foot:

| Year | Expenses/sf | Gross (no stop) | Modified gross (base-year stop) | NNN |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $9.50 | $18.50 | $18.50 | $19.00 |
| 2 | $9.88 | $18.12 | $18.50 | $19.00 |
| 3 | $10.28 | $17.72 | $18.50 | $19.00 |
| 4 | $10.69 | $17.31 | $18.50 | $19.00 |
| 5 | $11.11 | $16.89 | $18.50 | $19.00 |

On 5,000 square feet, year-five NOI is $84,433 for the unprotected gross lease versus $92,500 for the base-year stop: a gap of $8,067 a year and, at a 7% cap rate, about $115,000 of value.

## What the veteran sees

Year one looks identical across all three, which is exactly why inexperienced owners don't price the difference. The cost of the gross lease shows up in year three or four, and it never reverses. (The NNN tenant pays $28.50 all-in in year one, $19.00 of rent plus $9.50 of expenses, against $28.00 under the others. It is the only structure where the landlord's net doesn't move with expenses, and that protection is worth a small premium to the tenant's total cost.)

In calm years the difference is small. In years when insurance reprices or property taxes reset, it's large. A sudden 25% jump in expenses at the year-three level of $10.28 would add about $2.57 per square foot of cost to the gross-lease landlord immediately, or roughly $12,800 a year on this suite.

## Change one thing

Inflation runs 7% instead of 4%. By year five, expenses are $12.45 per square foot, and the unprotected gross landlord nets about $15.55, nearly $3 below year one, a 16% cut to NOI. The modified gross landlord is still at $18.50. Whenever you're offered a gross lease in an inflationary period, ask for the stop.

# Example 3: Reading a Strip Center's Rent Roll

## The setup

You're looking at a 46,000-square-foot neighborhood retail center with five tenants, all on NNN leases. You want to know how safe the income is.

## The numbers

| Tenant | Square feet | Rent/sf | Annual rent | Share of rent | Years left |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Grocery (anchor) | 28,000 | $11.00 | $308,000 | 42.4% | 4.5 |
| Pharmacy | 11,000 | $22.00 | $242,000 | 33.3% | 9.0 |
| Restaurant | 3,200 | $26.00 | $83,200 | 11.5% | 2.0 |
| Salon | 1,800 | $24.00 | $43,200 | 5.9% | 1.0 |
| Dry cleaner | 2,000 | $25.00 | $50,000 | 6.9% | 3.0 |
| **Total** | **46,000** | **$15.79** | **$726,400** | **100%** | |

Weighted-average lease term (by rent): 5.4 years. By square foot: 5.2 years.

Rent expiring within three years: restaurant, salon, and cleaner = $176,400, or 24.3% of rent (15.2% of square footage).

## What the veteran sees

WALT of 5.4 years looks comfortable. Look underneath: two tenants provide 75.7% of the rent, and the anchor's lease has 4.5 years to run. The grocer pays $11 per square foot, less than half what the small shops pay, but it's what brings the traffic. A quarter of rent rolls within three years, which is normal for small shops and manageable, since small-space leasing is quick and cheap.

The real risk is the grocer. If it leaves, you lose 42.4% of your rent and the traffic that supports everyone else. And that's before you read the pharmacy's lease for a co-tenancy clause.

## Change one thing

The pharmacy lease contains a clause: if the anchor goes dark for more than six months, the pharmacy pays 50% of its rent until a replacement opens. The grocer leaves at year 4.5, and the box sits empty for 18 months. You lose $308,000 of grocery rent plus $121,000 a year of reduced pharmacy rent, for a total of $429,000, or 59% of the center's income, plus about $700,000 ($25 per square foot) to build out the space for a new tenant. A building that looked like a 5.4-year bond now has a single point of failure, written in a clause you'd have missed if you only read the rent roll.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

You own an office building and sign a tenant to a full-service gross lease with no expense stop. Operating costs then rise faster than expected. Who bears the increase?

- [ ] The tenant, because it is responsible for the building it occupies :: In a full-service gross lease the tenant pays a single fixed rent. Without a stop or pass-through the tenant does not share in cost increases.
- [x] You, the landlord :: The tenant's rent is fixed and you pay every expense, so higher costs come straight out of your NOI. A base-year stop or NNN structure would shift the growth to the tenant.
- [ ] Both, split in proportion to the square footage leased :: A proportional share is how NNN and modified-gross leases allocate costs. A gross lease with no stop has no sharing mechanism at all.
- [ ] The lender, through the debt service coverage test on the loan :: A lender tests coverage but doesn't pay expenses. Rising costs reduce the cushion for the loan but the money comes out of the owner.

> The question to ask of any commercial lease is: when costs go up, whose pocket does it come out of?

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** A broker offers a single-tenant building at a 6.25% cap rate with seven years left on the lease. The rent is about 7% above market. You estimate renewal odds at 65%. If the tenant leaves, you'd spend roughly $285,000 in tenant improvements, $42,000 in commissions, and carry the empty building for a year, and the building's stabilized value would be well below your purchase price.

What is the best read of this offer?

- [ ] It's a 6.25% return, so compare it to other 6% to 6.5% investments. :: The cap rate is the return only if the lease renews at today's rent indefinitely. With above-market rent and rollover costs, the effective return is far lower.
- [x] Return depends on rollover, so price it and negotiate. :: Layer renewal odds and re-tenanting costs onto the cash flows and the effective unlevered return falls to the mid-3% range. If the seller needs 6.25%, you need a lower price.
- [ ] Avoid net lease investments, since rollover risk is always unacceptable. :: Net lease can be excellent when the price reflects the rollover and when the building has alternative uses. The problem here is the price relative to the risk.
- [ ] Assume 100% renewal because the tenant has been there for years. :: Past tenure raises odds but never to certainty, and a renewing tenant paying above-market rent will negotiate a lower rent. The asymmetric downside is where the money gets lost.

> A net lease is a bond that matures in a building. Value the building on the day the bond matures.

## Q3 | scenario

**Scenario.** You're reviewing a shopping center that is 42% leased to a grocery anchor and 33% to a pharmacy. The overall weighted-average lease term is a comfortable 5.4 years. The seller's package includes the rent roll and a summary of lease abstracts.

Which diligence step matters most before you rely on that WALT?

- [ ] Confirm that every tenant's rent is paid on the first of the month. :: Payment history is useful, but it speaks to the past. The risk here is structural and sits in the lease documents.
- [ ] Recalculate the WALT using square footage rather than rent. :: By square foot the WALT is 5.2 years instead of 5.4. It barely changes the picture and doesn't address how concentrated the income is.
- [x] Read every lease for co-tenancy, go-dark, and termination rights. :: The pharmacy's rent may drop if the grocer leaves. Those clauses can turn a long WALT into a short one, and only the leases themselves reveal them.
- [ ] Ask the seller what rents the tenants would pay for renewal. :: A seller's estimate of renewal rents is a sales assumption. Verify through market comps and estoppels, not seller optimism.

> A weighted average is a summary of the rent roll. The leases are the facts.

## Q4 | multiple_choice

A strip center has a WALT of 5.4 years. Why can a comfortable-looking WALT still hide serious risk?

- [ ] Because WALT is only meaningful for single-tenant properties :: WALT is useful for multi-tenant properties; it simply needs the rollover schedule and tenant concentration to give the full picture.
- [x] An average hides when rent expires and which tenants carry it. :: A quarter of rent can expire in three years, and two tenants can provide three-quarters of income. The average blends these into one calm number.
- [ ] Because WALT includes only tenants with options to renew :: WALT is built from remaining lease terms, typically excluding unexercised options. This isn't the source of the problem.
- [ ] Because WALT should be calculated on a square footage basis only :: Weighting by rent is usually more meaningful, since a dollar of rent matters more than a square foot. Neither weighting captures timing and concentration.

> Always pair the average with the shape: the rollover schedule and the top-tenant share.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** You have $600,000 to invest and about three months of living expenses in reserve beyond that. You're choosing between (1) a single-tenant building with 3 years left on a lease to a regional franchisee, priced at a 7.5% cap rate, and (2) a 16-unit apartment building, 95% occupied, at a 6.5% cap rate.

Which fits your situation better, and why?

- [ ] The single-tenant building, because the higher cap rate means more income :: The extra yield is compensation for risk you may not be able to bear. With 3 years left and a franchisee tenant, you could face zero income on short notice.
- [x] The apartments, because losing one tenant costs about 6% of income. :: Apartment risk is granular: any single vacancy is small and quickly re-leased. With thin reserves you want risk you can absorb in pieces rather than risk that arrives all at once.
- [ ] Neither, since a first purchase should wait until reserves are larger :: Caution is not unreasonable, but the question asks which fits better now. The apartments suit a modest reserve far better than a binary single-tenant exposure.
- [ ] Whichever has the lower purchase price per square foot :: Price per square foot ignores how the income can fail and what your reserves can survive. It would be a poor way to choose between very different risk profiles.

> Match the way an asset's income breaks to the way your balance sheet bends. Reserves decide which risks you can afford to own.
