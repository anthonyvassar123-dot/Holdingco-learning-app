# Lesson

I've made money on perhaps a dozen deals because I understood the debt better than the person across the table, and I've lost money on exactly two because I didn't. The second of those was a loan I'll never forget. It was a perfectly good ten-year fixed-rate loan, with a clean rate and a fine lender, and a prepayment clause I'd skimmed in the term sheet because the rate was so good. Four years in, a buyer showed up with an offer 12% above what I'd underwritten. Rates had fallen. The lender's yield-maintenance formula turned my sale into a $390,000 line item on the settlement statement. The buyer's price was great. After the prepayment penalty, I'd have been better off keeping the building.

Nothing was hidden. The clause was in the documents. I hadn't read the loan the way I read the lease or the rent roll. Debt is a contract with a start date, an end date, and a set of rules for what happens in between, and the rules decide who wins when things change.

## Where the money comes from

Different lenders are built for different jobs. The right question isn't "who has the lowest rate?" but "who is designed to lend on what I'm doing?"

**Banks and credit unions.** Local and regional lenders are flexible and relationship-driven. Terms are usually 3 to 10 years with 20 to 30-year amortization, often with recourse (a personal guarantee) and a rate that resets after five years. They are the right choice for smaller deals, for owners with other business at the bank, and for properties that don't fit an institutional box. Expect covenants: minimum DSCR, deposit requirements, reporting.

**Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac programs).** For stabilized apartment buildings, these are the gold standard: long terms (5, 7, 10 years or more), 30-year amortization, competitive fixed rates, non-recourse structures with standard carve-outs, and assumability. In exchange you accept reserves, inspections, and strict prepayment rules. Their appetite is for stabilized properties with a track record.

**Life insurance companies.** Conservative lenders with low rates and low leverage (50% to 65% LTV) on high-quality assets. They want the best properties and the strongest sponsors.

**CMBS (conduit) loans.** Loans pooled and sold to bond investors. Non-recourse, fixed rate, often interest-only, good leverage, and the most rigid servicing: when you need something changed, you deal with a special servicer, not a banker. Prepayment is typically by defeasance.

**Bridge and debt-fund loans.** Short-term (1 to 3 years, with extensions), floating-rate, interest-only, sized on a business plan. Built for properties in transition: renovation, lease-up, a recent acquisition. They cost more and require a take-out plan.

**Seller financing.** The seller carries part of the price. Terms are negotiable: the rate, term, amortization, and what happens at maturity are all on the table. Because the seller can have motives other than rate (tax deferral, a steady income), the best seller-financing terms are often better than any institutional offer.

**Assumable loans.** An existing loan you take over from the seller. If the loan carries a rate well below today's market, an assumption can be worth a lot. Watch the gap between the loan balance and the price: you'll need other money for it.

## Fixed versus floating

A fixed-rate loan has one rate for the whole term. Your payment is known, your DSCR is known, and your only risk is what happens at maturity. A floating-rate loan pays an index (these days a benchmark like SOFR) plus a spread, resetting monthly. The payment can move in either direction.

Floating-rate loans are sold on lower initial cost and flexibility (often no heavy prepayment penalty). They make sense when a business plan has a short horizon and a defined exit, such as renovating and refinancing in 24 to 36 months. Their danger is that they transfer rate risk to you precisely when your NOI is least stable.

If you borrow floating, you will almost always be required to buy an **interest rate cap**: a contract that pays you the difference whenever the index exceeds a strike rate. A cap doesn't lock your rate; it sets a ceiling on the index part. The spread on top is fixed. The price depends on the strike, the term, and volatility, and it's paid upfront, typically a half-percent to a few percent of the loan in volatile periods.

## Term, amortization, and the balloon

**Term** is how long until the loan is due. **Amortization** is the schedule used to calculate payments, often longer than the term. A 10-year term with 30-year amortization leaves about 85% of the principal still owing at maturity, a **balloon** you must refinance or pay by selling. That's where most borrowers get hurt: not in the payment, but in the maturity.

Match the loan term to the plan. If you intend to hold for ten years, take the ten-year loan. If your plan has an exit in four years, don't take the loan whose prepayment terms are built for ten.

## Prepayment: the clause that decides your exit

Every fixed-rate loan has a price for paying it off early. Four structures are common:

- **Step-down (e.g., 5-4-3-2-1):** a percentage of the balance, falling each year. Prepay in year 3 and it's 3%. Simple, predictable, and the best structure for borrowers who might sell.
- **Yield maintenance:** a formula that compensates the lender for lost interest if rates have fallen below your rate. It's roughly the present value of the interest difference between your coupon and the Treasury yield for the remaining term. If rates have *risen*, the penalty falls to a floor, commonly 1%. If rates have fallen, it can be enormous.
- **Defeasance:** you replace the loan's cash flows with a portfolio of Treasury bonds. The cost behaves like yield maintenance, with extra fees and complexity.
- **Open period / lockout:** loans often become prepayable at par in the final few months. Some begin with a lockout where you can't prepay at all.

The behavior to remember: yield maintenance and defeasance hurt most exactly when you'd most want to sell, after rates have fallen and values have risen.

## Recourse: whose money is behind the loan

**Recourse** means that if the property doesn't cover the loan, the lender can pursue your other assets. **Non-recourse** means the lender's remedy is limited to the property. Non-recourse loans nearly always contain **carve-outs** (the "bad boy" provisions) that turn them into full recourse if you do something you shouldn't: commit fraud, misapply insurance proceeds or rents, transfer the property without consent, or file for bankruptcy voluntarily. Read the carve-out list. The one that surprises people most is **failing to keep the borrower a single-purpose entity**.

Recourse isn't always bad. Banks that require a guarantee often give better terms and more flexibility. But you should treat a personal guarantee as what it is: your house.

## A term sheet, line by line, with real costs

When a lender sends a term sheet, price every line. On a $3,000,000 loan, the costs I'd expect to see in the mid-2020s look like this, give or take with the lender and the market:

| Item | Typical | On $3,000,000 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Origination fee | 0.5% to 1.0% | $15,000 to $30,000 |
| Lender legal and third-party reports (appraisal, environmental, property condition) | $20,000 to $45,000 | $20,000 to $45,000 |
| Extension fee (bridge loans), each | 0.25% to 0.5% | $7,500 to $15,000 |
| Rate-lock or good-faith deposit | 0.5% to 1.0% | $15,000 to $30,000 (credited at closing) |
| Interest and tax escrows, replacement reserve | several months of payments | often $75,000 to $150,000 held, not spent |

Two points about these. First, the fees are not the real cost of a loan; they are the cost of *getting* it. The real cost is what the structure does to you in year four. Second, escrows and reserves are your money, but it is trapped, so it counts against your return and your liquidity.

You'll also be offered **points**: pay 1% of the loan upfront ($30,000 on $3 million) to lower the rate, usually by 0.125% to 0.25%. Do the breakeven. A 0.25% reduction saves roughly $7,500 a year on $3 million of interest, so $30,000 pays back in four years. If you expect to hold the loan for ten, points are a good trade. If you're likely to sell in three, they're a gift to the lender.

## The maturity wall

Most borrowers think about the payment. Lenders think about the maturity. A 10-year loan on a 30-year schedule at 6.5% leaves about 85% of the original balance due at the end, which on $3,000,000 is roughly $2.54 million. That balloon is what you must refinance or repay by selling. Whether you can do that depends on the property's NOI in year ten, the rates in year ten, and the lending standards in year ten, none of which you know today. The conservative owner sizes the original loan so that even at a materially higher rate, a refinance of the balloon still works, and pays down principal when cash allows. A clean maturity is worth more than a clever rate.

## The mistakes I see most

**Shopping on rate alone.** The cheapest rate with the harshest prepayment clause is often the most expensive loan if you sell before maturity. Price the whole structure, including the exit.

**Taking a maturity that doesn't fit the plan.** A five-year loan with a three-year plan sounds like cushion; a three-year loan on a five-year plan is a refinance you can't control.

**Skipping the rate cap, or buying a cap with a strike far above where you can survive.** A cap that doesn't protect your DSCR is decoration.

**Ignoring covenants.** Cash management triggers, DSCR tests, and required reporting have forced more owners into defaults than bad properties have.

**Assuming you'll refinance.** Plans that depend on a refinance at today's rates and today's loan sizes are plans that depend on the market. Ask what happens if the takeout loan is 20% smaller.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Pay up for the structure that lets you sell.** The extra quarter-point for a step-down instead of yield maintenance is cheap insurance for an owner who may want to sell in three to five years. I'll pay it nearly every time on a deal I might trade.

**Think about the lender as a partner you can't fire.** A bank that knows you and answers the phone is worth a little extra rate. A CMBS servicer who needs three signatures to approve a roof is worth a lot less than the rate savings suggest.

**When the seller offers financing, ask why.** Sometimes it's tax planning or income. Sometimes the seller knows something about the property that scares conventional lenders. Both are information.

## If you remember nothing else

- Read the whole loan, not just the rate: term, amortization, prepayment, recourse, covenants, and reserves decide your outcome.
- Match the loan to the plan: floating for short, defined transitions; fixed for holds; step-down prepayment if you might sell.
- A rate cap is only useful if its strike keeps the deal alive. Price it, and model your worst year.

# Example 1: Fixed, Floating, and the Cap

## The setup

You're borrowing $3,000,000 on a property with $300,000 of NOI. Option A is a 10-year fixed loan at 6.50% with 30-year amortization. Option B is a 3-year floating bridge loan, interest-only, at the index (currently 4.30%) plus a 3.25% spread. Option B comes with a requirement to buy a 3-year rate cap with a 5.0% strike on the index, which costs $40,000 upfront.

## The numbers

Option A: annual debt service is $227,544 (constant 7.585%). Over three years you pay $575,151 of interest and $107,483 of principal.

Option B: total interest over three years depends on where the index goes.

| Index path (years 1, 2, 3) | Unprotected cost | With the 5.0% cap (incl. $40,000) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Flat (4.30%, 4.30%, 4.30%) | $679,500 | $719,500 |
| Rises (4.30%, 5.80%, 6.30%) | $784,500 | $761,500 |
| Falls (4.30%, 3.30%, 3.30%) | $619,500 | $659,500 |

All-in rate in year 3 if rates rise: 6.30% + 3.25% = 9.55% without a cap (interest $286,500, DSCR 1.05); with the cap, the index is limited to 5.0% and the all-in rate to 8.25% (interest $247,500, DSCR 1.21).

## What the veteran sees

On interest alone, the fixed loan ($575,151) is cheaper than the floating loan in every path, even the one where the index falls ($619,500). Floating looks cheaper only against the fixed loan's total payments of $682,633, and that comparison flatters it: $107,483 of those payments is principal, which is equity you keep.

The cap does what it's for. In the rising scenario it saves $23,000 net of its cost and keeps year-3 DSCR at 1.21 instead of 1.05. In the other two it's a $40,000 premium. That's the nature of insurance. It's cheap to hold in good years and priceless in the one bad year that would have forced a sale.

The honest comparison is this: floating-rate debt makes sense when the business plan produces a *step change* in NOI inside the loan term (renovation, lease-up), and the structure protects you if rates and the plan both go wrong. If the property is stable and you plan to hold, the fixed loan costs a little more in some years and lets you sleep.

## Change one thing

Suppose the cap has a 6.5% strike instead of 5.0%, and it costs $18,000 instead of $40,000. In the rising scenario, the index reaches 6.3% and never exceeds the strike, so the cap never pays. You've spent $18,000 for protection that doesn't engage until the index is above where your deal breaks. A cheaper cap is not a better deal if its strike sits above your breakeven rate.

# Example 2: What a Prepayment Clause Costs

## The setup

You have a $4,000,000 loan, 10-year term, 5.0% fixed, 30-year amortization. After three years, the balance is **$3,813,744**. You get an offer to sell, which would require paying off the loan with seven years remaining. Compare the cost under a step-down and under yield maintenance, and see how it depends on where Treasury yields are when you sell.

## The numbers

Step-down (5-4-3-2-1): you sell during year 3, so the penalty is 3%.

| Structure | Treasury yield at sale | Cost to prepay | % of balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Step-down 5-4-3-2-1 | any | $114,412 | 3.0% |
| Yield maintenance | 5.5% (rates rose) | $38,137 (1% floor) | 1.0% |
| Yield maintenance | 4.25% | $161,737 | 4.2% |
| Yield maintenance | 3.50% | $331,538 | 8.7% |
| Yield maintenance | 3.00% | $449,426 | 11.8% |

Yield maintenance here is the present value of the remaining payments and balloon at the 5.0% coupon, discounted at the Treasury yield, minus the balance.

## What the veteran sees

Yield maintenance is an option the lender holds against you. If rates rise, you pay almost nothing; if rates fall, you pay dearly, and rates falling is when property values are usually rising, when you'd most want to sell. At a 3.0% Treasury yield, the penalty is $449,426, nearly 12% of the balance.

The step-down is a known quantity: $114,412 whatever the market does. If you're likely to sell inside the term, the step-down's certainty is worth more than a lower rate. What rate premium is worth paying? Assume the yield-maintenance loan has a rate 0.25% lower: on a $3.8 million balance, that's $9,500 a year, or $28,500 over three years. The step-down's worst case is $114,000, but the yield-maintenance worst case is $450,000. Paying $28,500 to cap the exposure is a bargain.

## Change one thing

You hold the loan seven more years to maturity. Yield maintenance falls to zero as the term runs out, and the step-down ends after year five. For a true ten-year hold, the only thing that matters is the rate and the balloon, and the cheaper yield-maintenance loan wins. The structure you choose should be the structure that matches the plan you have, not the one you'd like to have.

# Example 3: Is the Assumable Loan Worth It?

## The setup

You're buying a 36-unit building for **$5,000,000** with NOI of $330,000. The seller has an assumable loan: $3,000,000 balance, 4.25% fixed, 25 years of amortization remaining, maturing in 6 years. The assumption fee is 1% ($30,000). A new market loan at 6.75% could be had for 70% of price ($3,500,000) on a 30-year amortization.

## The numbers

| Item | Assume the loan | New 70% loan |
|---|---:|---:|
| Loan amount | $3,000,000 | $3,500,000 |
| Rate | 4.25% | 6.75% |
| Annual debt service | $195,026 | $272,411 |
| Equity gap vs. new loan | $500,000 more equity | none |
| If gap filled with a second loan at 11% interest-only | +$55,000 | |
| Total annual debt cost | $250,026 | $272,411 |
| DSCR | 1.32 | 1.21 |

Annual savings from assumption (with gap financing): $22,385. If the gap were filled with your own cash instead, the savings would be $77,385 a year on $500,000 of equity (a 15.5% return on the extra money). Same-size comparison: $3,000,000 at 4.25% costs $38,470 less per year than $3,000,000 at 6.75%, which has a present value of about $177,840 over six years at an 8% discount rate, against a $30,000 fee.

## What the veteran sees

The assumption is worth real money: roughly $150,000 of present value on the same-size loan, and meaningfully better coverage (1.32 versus 1.21). The cost is the equity gap and the risk it carries. If you fill it with 11% money, you still come out about $22,000 a year ahead. You're breaking even against the second loan at a gap-financing cost of 15.5%.

Then the maturity. In six years the assumed loan balance is $2,539,426, and you'll refinance it at whatever the market is then. Below-market debt is a depreciating asset. The question is whether you're buying six years of savings or something you expect to hold for twenty. If your plan is six years, the assumption is a very good deal. If you plan to hold for twenty, it's a good start but not the whole story.

## Change one thing

The lender won't permit secondary financing behind the assumed loan, so the $500,000 gap has to be equity from you or partners. Now you're deciding whether $500,000 of additional capital earns more than a 15.5% return on its own deployment. For most investors in most periods, it doesn't: that's a high hurdle, and the assumption still wins. But if partners expect a 12% return on that capital, remember to put their cost into the comparison too. A deal with a larger equity check isn't free just because the loan is cheap.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

A lender requires you to buy an interest rate cap with a 5.0% strike on a floating-rate loan at index plus 3.25%. What does the cap do?

- [ ] It fixes your total interest rate at 5.0% for the term :: The cap limits the index, not your total rate, and it doesn't fix anything. You still pay the spread on top of the index.
- [x] It limits the index part to 5.0%, so your all-in rate tops out at 8.25% :: The cap pays you when the index exceeds the strike. Your cost is capped at strike plus spread (5.0% + 3.25%) while still benefiting if the index falls.
- [ ] It guarantees that you will never pay more than the original rate :: A cap protects only above its strike. The rate can still rise, up to the ceiling, and the upfront premium is a cost you pay regardless.
- [ ] It reduces the spread charged by the lender :: The spread is set in the loan terms. A cap is a separate contract with a separate provider, and it doesn't change the spread.

> A cap is insurance on the index. Check that the strike keeps your DSCR alive, not that it's merely cheap.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** You may sell a building in three to four years. A lender offers two 10-year fixed loans on a $3.8 million balance: one at 5.50% with yield-maintenance prepayment, and another at 5.75% with a 3-2-1 step-down after year three. The rate difference costs about $9,500 a year.

Which loan is the better fit for your plan?

- [ ] The 5.50% yield-maintenance loan, since the lower rate saves money every year :: The savings are modest, about $9,500 a year, but the yield-maintenance penalty can reach hundreds of thousands if rates fall before you sell.
- [x] The 5.75% step-down loan, since its exit cost is known and capped. :: The extra rate is a small, certain cost. The step-down limits your sale exposure to a few percent of the balance, which protects your sale price.
- [ ] Neither, since all fixed-rate loans penalize early sale. :: Prepayment structures differ a great deal. A step-down is far more forgiving than yield maintenance and can be planned around.
- [ ] Either, since the cost over a few years is the same. :: The costs are nowhere near equal in a falling-rate sale. Yield maintenance can cost many times the step-down's penalty.

> The best loan for an owner who may sell isn't the cheapest loan. It's the one with the exit you can price.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

You have a non-recourse loan with standard carve-outs. Which of the following could convert it to full personal recourse?

- [ ] A drop in NOI that causes a DSCR default :: A poor-performing property is the lender's risk under a non-recourse loan. Underperformance alone does not trigger personal liability.
- [ ] A decline in the market value of the property :: Value declines are the collateral risk the lender accepted when it chose non-recourse.
- [x] Transferring the property without the lender's consent :: Unauthorized transfers, fraud, misapplied rents or insurance proceeds, and voluntary bankruptcy are classic carve-outs that remove the non-recourse protection.
- [ ] Missing one monthly payment by a few days :: A late payment may trigger fees or a default notice, but a simple payment default is the situation non-recourse protection exists for.

> Non-recourse means the lender looks to the property unless you misbehave. Know the list of misbehaviors.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** You're pricing a plan to renovate a building over 18 months and refinance. A bridge lender offers $3.0 million at index + 3.25%, interest-only, 3 years. NOI is $300,000 today, with a projected $345,000 after renovation. The lender requires a cap at a 5.0% strike. The index is 4.30%. Your broker argues the cap is "a waste of money" because rates are going down.

What is the best response?

- [ ] Skip the cap by negotiating with the lender, since forecasts say rates will fall. :: Nobody reliably forecasts rates. The cap is what lets you survive the case where the forecast is wrong.
- [x] Keep the cap, and confirm that the strike keeps year-three DSCR near 1.2 in the rising-rate case. :: At an 8.25% all-in ceiling, interest is $247,500 against $300,000 of NOI, a DSCR of 1.21. That's the test of whether the cap protects the deal.
- [ ] Buy a cap with a much higher strike to cut the upfront cost. :: A higher strike lowers the price but moves the protection above where your deal breaks. At a 6.5% strike the cap would not engage in the rising path.
- [ ] Switch to a fixed-rate loan, because floating debt is never appropriate. :: Floating debt suits a defined transition when the plan includes a take-out. The point is to structure the risk, not to avoid it by principle.

> Insurance is judged by the worst case it covers, not by how rarely you claim on it.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** A seller's assumable loan is $3.0 million at 4.25%, saving about $38,000 a year versus a new 6.75% loan of the same size. A new 70% loan would be $3.5 million, so assuming leaves you needing $500,000 more equity. A second loan to fill the gap costs 11% interest-only. The assumed loan matures in six years with a balance of about $2.54 million.

What is the best read of this opportunity?

- [x] Take the assumption, but plan for the maturity in six years. :: Even with an 11% second loan, the annual debt cost is lower by about $22,000 and coverage improves from 1.21 to 1.32. The savings end at maturity, so a six-year plan is the one to underwrite.
- [ ] Pass, because the extra $500,000 of equity makes the deal too expensive. :: The extra capital earns the debt savings, and the gap financing is cheaper than the break-even cost of about 15.5%. The extra equity is part of the trade, not a veto.
- [ ] Pass, because below-market debt is always worthless after the first refinance. :: It's valuable during its life. Six years of lower payments is worth roughly $150,000 in present value on the same-size loan.
- [ ] Take it only if the seller pays the assumption fee. :: A $30,000 fee is small next to roughly $178,000 of present-value savings, so it shouldn't decide the question.

> Cheap debt is worth owning for as long as it lasts. Figure out what your plan looks like when it ends.
