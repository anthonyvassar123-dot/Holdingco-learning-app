# Lesson

The first value-add deal I ever did went wrong in a way I've seen a hundred times since. I bought a 24-unit building with dated interiors. The broker's pitch was simple: renovate every unit, charge $200 a month more, and the building is worth $450,000 more. The math was fine. The execution wasn't. I planned to renovate eight units at a time, took four months longer than budgeted on the first batch, found that the rents I'd promised in my model needed granite counters the tenants didn't care about, and discovered that my contractor's "allowance" for flooring didn't include the subfloor under three kitchens. The renovation cost came in 28% over. The premium I could actually get was $120, not $200. The project still made money. It made about a third of what the model said.

Value-add is the part of real estate where you can do the most to create returns and the part where optimism does the most damage. This module is about doing the math the way a skeptic would, so you only take the projects that survive it.

## What "value-add" really means

Value-add means buying a property that is under-earning relative to what it could earn, and spending money and effort to close the gap. The gap can come from a few places:

- **Physical:** dated interiors, deferred curb appeal, a laundry room that doesn't work.
- **Operational:** below-market rents, high vacancy, poor collections, no bill-back of utilities, bloated expenses.
- **Positioning:** a building aimed at the wrong tenant, or a poorly managed property in a good location.

Notice that two of those three cost little or nothing. The best value-add deals often start with operations, because fixing management costs less than renovating and returns faster. The expensive one, the physical renovation, is where the math needs to be most rigorous, because you're spending real dollars up front for a premium you hope to collect later.

## The unit-level return

The core question for any interior renovation: *what does one dollar spent earn in extra rent, every year?*

*Return on cost = (monthly rent premium × 12) / all-in cost per unit*

"All-in" is where people cheat. It must include the direct renovation cost, the lost rent while the unit is down for the extra time the work takes, any leasing cost, and a share of overhead. A $9,500 interior renovation that adds 20 days of downtime on a $1,075 unit actually costs $9,500 + $717 = $10,217. A $135 premium on that is 15.9%, not the 17.1% you'd get from the brochure math.

Then compare that return to the going cap rate. If the market pays a 6.5% cap rate for stabilized income, then each dollar of extra NOI is worth $15.38 of building value. A renovation that returns 15.9% on cost creates, at a 6.5% cap, about $2.44 of value per dollar spent. That's the "forced appreciation" the brochures talk about. It's real, but it only exists if the premium is real.

**The target.** I look for a return on cost of at least 12% to 15% on a unit-level renovation, which means a spread of 5.5 to 8.5 points over the cap rate. Anything under about 10% isn't worth the execution risk, because overruns and a smaller-than-expected premium will eat the cushion quickly.

## How to find the premium without fooling yourself

The premium is the number that determines everything, and it's the number the broker will overstate. To estimate it honestly:

1. **Find renovated comps** in the same submarket and compare them to unrenovated units in the same or similar buildings. The difference, adjusted for size and amenities, is the market's price for your kind of renovation.
2. **Test it with a model unit.** Renovate one or two units first and lease them. Real tenants paying real rent beat a spreadsheet. If you can't lease the model unit at the premium within 30 days, you've learned something for the cost of two units.
3. **Beware the ceiling.** From Module 2: premium rents still sit under the affordability ceiling. If your tenants are at 33% of income already, a $135 bump is a harder sell.
4. **Don't renovate past the market.** Granite counters in a building where tenants pay $1,075 are overbuilt. The market pays for a finished, clean, modern look: durable flooring, paint, fixtures, appliances. It doesn't pay for the features that make you feel good.

## Budgets, soft costs, and contingency

A real renovation budget has more than the line items:

- **Hard costs:** labor and materials.
- **Soft costs:** permits, design, inspections, project management. Plan 3% to 8% of hard costs.
- **Contingency:** the money for what you don't know yet. On a building with unknown conditions (any building built before the 1990s), plan 10% to 15%. On a gut rehab, 15% to 20%. A contingency of 3% to 5% is a decoration.
- **Carry costs:** the interest, taxes, and insurance on the building while you're renovating, and the lost rent from downtime.

In the mid-2020s, a typical light interior renovation for an apartment ran $6,000 to $12,000 per unit, depending on scope and region. Exterior and common-area work often adds $1,000 to $3,000 per unit. These are broad ranges; get three bids from contractors who've done similar work.

Always add a line for **deferred maintenance** separately from value-add. The roof, the sewer line, the boiler: those don't generate a premium. They are the price of owning the building. Confusing them with value-add inflates the return on the projects that actually earn one.

## Phasing: you can't renovate what's occupied

A renovation plan has to work with the tenants who live there. The most common approach is renovating units **at natural turnover**: when a tenant leaves, you renovate before re-renting. In an apartment building with 40% annual turnover, a 36-unit property turns about 14 units a year, so a full interior program takes two and a half years. That's slow, but it's kind to occupancy and to your relationship with tenants.

The alternative is accelerating: choosing not to renew leases, paying tenants to move, or renovating occupied units with their consent. That brings the premium sooner but costs vacancy, relocation, and sometimes goodwill, and in some jurisdictions it triggers tenant protections. Do the math on both. A faster timeline pays for itself only if the extra premium arrives sooner than the cost of the disruption.

## Other income and expense levers

The cheapest value-add is often not a renovation at all:

- **Utility bill-back (RUBS):** Ratio Utility Billing Systems pass a share of water, sewer, and trash costs to tenants. A building paying $85 a unit per month in utilities that recovers 80% adds $68 per unit per month of income.
- **Fees:** pet rent, parking, storage, application fees, late fees. Each is small. Together they can add $20 to $50 a unit a month.
- **Expense reduction:** re-bidding insurance, negotiating service contracts, appealing property taxes, and fixing water leaks. A 10% cut in a $140,000 expense line is $14,000 of NOI, or about $215,000 of value at a 6.5% cap.
- **Professional management and leasing:** better collections and faster turns are not glamorous, but 2 points of occupancy on a $700,000 income line is $14,000 a year.

One caution on bill-backs: they raise the tenant's *total* housing cost. A $68 monthly utility charge on a $1,075 rent is a 6.3% increase, even if the posted rent didn't change. Tenants notice. Test the total cost against rent-to-income, and phase the program in at lease renewals.

## Reading a scope of work

When a contractor hands you a bid, read it the way you'd read a lease. Is it a lump sum or time-and-materials? What's excluded (permits, subfloor repairs, hazardous-material abatement, disposal fees)? What are the payment terms: a deposit of 10% is normal, 50% upfront is a warning. What's the schedule, and what happens if the contractor is late? Does it include a one-year warranty on workmanship? In the unit renovation above, a $2,400 flooring line sounds like a plan until you notice that the bid says "allowance" and not "installed." An allowance is a number the contractor expects you to exceed. Ask for installed prices per unit, per square foot of flooring, per appliance, so you can compare three bids line by line.

I also ask contractors one question: *"What's the thing on this job most likely to go wrong?"* The good ones answer immediately and specifically: "The subfloor under the kitchens in the older units, so I'd hold $1,500 a unit." The ones who say "nothing, we do these all the time" are the ones who send change orders.

## Rent growth is two numbers, not one

When you model rent growth on a value-add, split it into the renovation premium and the market growth that comes on top. A $135 premium doesn't stop the market from moving: if comparable rents grow 3% a year, your renovated unit at $1,210 should become $1,246 next year, and your unrenovated unit at $1,075 should become $1,107. The premium is a spread you capture once at the time of renovation, and then it grows with the market. A common modeling error is to add the premium on top of an assumed 5% annual growth, which double-counts the rent increase. Keep them separate, and write down which one you're counting.

## The mistakes I see most

**Underestimating the cost.** Every renovation has surprises. The only question is whether your budget has room for them.

**Overestimating the premium.** The most common error by far. Get proof from comps and model units, not from the seller's broker.

**Ignoring downtime.** Each extra week a unit is empty is a week of rent you don't get. Include it in the return.

**Renovating too much.** Spending $15,000 to achieve a premium that $9,000 would have achieved. The last dollars of a renovation usually earn less than the first.

**Doing it all at once with borrowed money.** If your plan depends on all renovations finishing and leasing at the target on a fixed schedule, with a floating-rate loan running, you've built a plan with no slack.

**Confusing deferred maintenance with value-add.** A new roof prevents leaks. It doesn't raise rents.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Who is the contractor?** I'd rather have a mediocre building with a great contractor than the reverse. A reliable renovator who shows up and finishes on schedule is worth a real premium. Ask for references and visit completed jobs.

**When to stop.** There's a point where the next unit costs the same but the premium is smaller, because the best units were done first. Stop when the marginal return falls under your hurdle.

**The renovation you don't do.** Sometimes the best value-add decision is to leave a building alone and run it well. If the numbers show a 9% return on cost, put the money into reserves or the next deal.

## If you remember nothing else

- Return on cost = annual premium / all-in cost, including downtime. Aim for 12% to 15% or better.
- A 3% contingency is decoration. Use 10% to 15% on any building with unknowns, and prove the premium with a model unit.
- The cheapest value-add is often operational: expenses, fees, bill-backs, management.

# Example 1: One Unit, Four Premiums

## The setup

At Maple Court (16 units, average rent $1,075), you plan a light interior renovation. The scope: luxury vinyl plank flooring ($2,400), cabinet refinishing, hardware and countertops ($3,200), a stainless appliance package ($2,300), lighting and fixtures ($700), and a bathroom refresh ($900). The work adds about 20 days of downtime beyond normal turnover.

## The numbers

Direct cost: $2,400 + $3,200 + $2,300 + $700 + $900 = **$9,500**.
Lost rent from extra downtime: $1,075 × 20 / 30 = **$717**.
All-in cost per unit: **$10,217**.

| Monthly premium | Annual premium | Return on all-in cost | Value created at a 6.5% cap | Value created net of cost |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $75 | $900 | 8.8% | $13,846 | $3,629 |
| $100 | $1,200 | 11.7% | $18,462 | $8,245 |
| $135 | $1,620 | 15.9% | $24,923 | $14,706 |
| $175 | $2,100 | 20.6% | $32,308 | $22,091 |

The premium needed for a 12% return is **$102** a month; for a 15% return, **$128**.

## What the veteran sees

The project works at $135 and fails my hurdle at $100. The difference is $35 a month, which is within the error bar of any rent survey. That tells you the project has almost no room for being wrong about the premium: if the true figure is $100, the return is 11.7%, below the 12% to 15% I'd want.

Run the whole building. Sixteen units cost $163,467 all-in. At $135 they add $25,920 a year of rent, or about $24,624 of NOI after a 5% management fee, and create $378,831 of value at a 6.5% cap (a $215,364 gain after cost). At $100, they add $19,200 a year and create about $280,615 of value (a $117,000 gain). Between the two scenarios, the value created differs by $98,000. That's the cost of being wrong about $35.

## Change one thing

Trim the scope. Skip the countertops and bathroom refresh and spend $6,400 instead of $9,500 (direct), plus the $717 downtime: $7,117 all-in. If the market pays $100 for the lighter finish, the return is $1,200 / $7,117 = 16.9%. The cheaper scope earns more at a smaller premium. The more important lesson is to price each component: ask what each piece of the renovation adds to rent, and drop the items that don't pay for themselves.

# Example 2: Budget, Ramp, and Overrun

## The setup

Willow Court is a 36-unit building. Your value-add program covers interior renovations for all 36 units ($9,500 each), exterior painting and siding repair ($48,000), landscaping and signage ($22,000), and a laundry room upgrade ($18,000). The roof and sewer work, which came out of diligence, are budgeted separately as deferred maintenance. You plan to renovate interiors at turnover (about 40% a year) and add $16,000 of other income from fees and bill-backs.

## The numbers

| Budget item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Interiors (36 × $9,500) | $342,000 |
| Exterior | $48,000 |
| Landscaping and signage | $22,000 |
| Laundry room | $18,000 |
| **Hard costs** | **$430,000** |
| Contingency (15%) | $64,500 |
| Soft costs (3%) | $12,900 |
| **Total budget** | **$507,400** |

The interior premium is $135 a month, or $1,620 a year per unit. With 14 units renovated in year 1, 14 in year 2, and 8 in year 3, the ramp in rent uplift is:

| Year | Units renovated (cumulative at year end) | Premium collected |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 14 | $11,340 |
| 2 | 28 | $34,020 |
| 3 | 36 | $51,840 |
| 4 | 36 | $58,320 |

Stabilized uplift: 36 × $1,620 + $16,000 other income = $74,320. After a 5% management fee ($3,716), NOI rises by **$70,604**.

| Result at stabilization | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Added value at a 6.5% cap ($70,604 / 0.065) | $1,086,215 |
| Total budget | $507,400 |
| Value created net of cost | $578,815 |
| Yield on cost ($70,604 / $507,400) | 13.9% |

## What the veteran sees

The base case works well: a 13.9% yield on cost against a 6.5% market cap rate is a spread of 7.4 points. But look at the timeline: the full uplift arrives only in year 4. In years 1 and 2 you're carrying a loan on a building that has earned only 16% and 48% of its eventual premium. A plan that makes sense at stabilization can still be a cash squeeze in the middle. Check that your debt service and reserves cover the ramp, not just the destination.

Now stress it.

| Scenario | Total cost | Yield on cost | Value created net of cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Base case | $507,400 | 13.9% | $578,815 |
| Hard costs run 25% over (contingency exhausted) | $550,400 | 12.8% | $535,815 |
| Premium only $100 (not $135) | $507,400 | 11.1% | $357,831 |
| Both | $550,400 | 10.2% | $314,831 |

## Change one thing

Run the cost overrun and the lower premium together and the yield falls to 10.2%, still above the cap rate but below my 12% hurdle, and the value created has dropped from $578,815 to $314,831, a 46% decline. Neither error looked large on its own. This is why contingency exists and why a model unit comes first: the two most common mistakes tend to arrive together.

# Example 3: The Utility Bill-Back

## The setup

At Maple Court, the owner pays water, sewer, and trash: about $85 per unit per month, or $16,320 a year for 16 units. You're considering a ratio utility billing program (RUBS) that would recover 80% of this from tenants, phased in as leases renew.

## The numbers

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Owner-paid utilities (16 × $85 × 12) | $16,320 |
| Recovery at 80% | $13,056 |
| Per unit per month | $68 |
| Value created at a 6.5% cap | $200,862 |
| Increase in tenant's total monthly housing cost | 6.3% on $1,075 |

Rent-to-income check with a renter income of $50,000: before, $1,075 × 12 / $50,000 = 25.8%; after, $1,143 × 12 / $50,000 = 27.4%.

## What the veteran sees

This is the best kind of value-add: no construction, a small cost to implement, and $200,000 of value. But look at the tenant's side. Their total cost rose 6.3%, more than the typical annual rent increase, and the rent-to-income ratio reaches 27.4%. In a market where tenants have room (below 30%), they'll accept it. In a market that's already at 33%, they will not, and turnover will cost more than the billback earns.

Implementation matters. Do it at lease renewal, not mid-lease. Offer a smaller base-rent increase in the same year. Explain it in writing. And check local law: some jurisdictions restrict or require disclosures for utility charges.

## Change one thing

Turnover rises from 40% to 48% as a result of the program, because a few tenants find cheaper places. On 16 units, that's about one extra turnover, costing about $3,500 including downtime. You collect an extra $13,056 and lose $3,500 to turnover, so the net gain is still $9,556 a year. The program survives a noticeable turnover increase. It would not survive a fifth of the building leaving. Watch retention in the first year, and be ready to adjust the pace or the amount if you see it slipping.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

You plan a $9,500 interior renovation that adds 20 days of downtime on a $1,075 unit and expect a $135 monthly rent premium. Which figure best represents the return on cost?

- [ ] About 17.1%, since $1,620 divided by $9,500 is the whole story, and downtime is a cost of doing business :: This uses direct cost only. The unit is also empty for extra days, which is a real cost of the project and lowers the return.
- [x] About 15.9% on $10,217 all-in :: Direct cost plus about $717 of extra lost rent gives $10,217. The annual premium of $1,620 divided by that is 15.9%.
- [ ] About 12.6%, based on a lower premium of $100 per month that the scenario never mentions :: A $100 premium would produce a different return, but nothing in the scenario says the premium is $100. The stated premium is $135.
- [ ] About 1.4%, since the monthly premium is $135 divided by $9,500 :: This divides a monthly figure by an annual-scale cost. The premium needs to be annualized first to compare it with the one-time spend.

> Return on cost counts everything you spend and everything you lose while the work is done.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** Your proforma assumes a $135 monthly premium for renovated units at an all-in cost of $10,217. A broker's pitch relies on it, but your own comps and a model unit suggest the market will pay about $100. Your hurdle for renovation projects is a 12% return on cost.

What is your best response?

- [ ] Proceed with the full scope, since 11.7% is close enough to 12% and the broker's number is similar :: Close enough leaves no room for overruns. A project at its hurdle on the base case has no margin for being wrong on cost.
- [ ] Raise the assumed premium back up to $135, because you believe the market is improving this year :: Assuming away the gap defeats the purpose of testing it. The model unit and comps are the evidence you have.
- [x] Trim the scope and re-test the return at the lower cost. :: At $100 and a scope costing around $7,100 all-in, the return is about 17%. Cutting items that don't earn their cost can rescue a project.
- [ ] Cancel the program entirely, because any project that falls below its hurdle should be dropped :: A project below the hurdle at one scope may clear it at another. Pricing each component is the better first move than abandoning the whole idea.

> The question isn't whether to renovate. It's which pieces of the renovation earn their cost.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

You're renovating a building constructed in the 1970s with some unknown conditions behind the walls. What is the most defensible contingency on hard costs?

- [ ] 3%, since the contractor has already quoted a firm price for the entire scope of work :: A firm price covers the contractor's scope, but not the unknowns, such as subfloor rot or old wiring. Three percent is too thin for a building with unknown conditions.
- [ ] 25% to 30%, to be safe in all circumstances and against any surprise that could turn up :: That is more than the usual range, and it would make many sound projects look unprofitable. It's a figure for a gut rehab at its most uncertain.
- [x] 10% to 15%, since old buildings hide problems. :: This is the common range for a building with unknowns. Higher is more appropriate for gut rehabs and lower for new construction.
- [ ] Zero, since contingency is a concept used only by developers and not by owners :: Any renovation encounters surprises. Without contingency, each one becomes a cash call.

> Contingency is not a pad. It is the cost of the surprises that every renovation has.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** You own a 36-unit building with 40% annual turnover and plan to renovate every interior. You could renovate at natural turnover over about 2.5 years, or decline lease renewals and renovate everything within 9 months. The faster path would pull forward about $50,000 of premium income but would cost about $90,000 in extra vacancy and relocation, and some risk to your reputation with remaining tenants.

Which approach is the better default?

- [ ] Accelerate, because pulling forward income always improves returns on the project overall :: Pulling income forward helps only if it exceeds the cost of doing so. Here the disruption costs more than the extra income it produces.
- [x] Renovate at turnover, starting with a few model units. :: The $90,000 of disruption exceeds the $50,000 pulled forward. A phased approach also lets the first results guide the rest of the program.
- [ ] Renovate only the units that are vacant and never touch the occupied ones, however long it takes :: This leaves units un-renovated indefinitely. Natural turnover eventually captures all of them at no disruption cost.
- [ ] Ask each tenant to pay for their own renovation through a higher monthly rent increase :: Tenants do not generally pay for the owner's improvements, and such an arrangement raises legal and relationship issues.

> Speed is only worth buying when it costs less than it earns.

## Q5 | multiple_choice

A utility bill-back program adds $68 a month to each tenant's total cost on a $1,075 rent. Why should you test it against the rent-to-income ratio before implementing?

- [x] It raises total housing cost, which may exceed what tenants can afford. :: A $68 charge is a 6.3% increase. If renters are already near 30% of income, the program can raise turnover and bad debt enough to offset the gain.
- [ ] Because utility bill-backs are illegal in most markets and can bring penalties for the owner :: They're permitted in most places, though some jurisdictions restrict or require disclosures. That's a reason to check local law, not to avoid the test.
- [ ] Because the bill-back reduces the property's NOI by adding costs to the operating statement :: A bill-back increases NOI by recovering costs. The concern is its effect on tenants, not on the statement.
- [ ] Because tenants can only pay the charge if the base rents also rise at the same time :: Rents don't have to rise for a bill-back to work. The test is whether tenants can absorb the total cost, not whether rents go up.

> Income that your tenants can't afford isn't income. Check the total cost they carry.
