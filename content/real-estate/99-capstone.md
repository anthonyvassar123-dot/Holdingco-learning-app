# Lesson

This is the case you've been preparing for. It's a single deal, worked from the first phone call to the day you decide whether to sell, and at each stage there's a decision that earlier modules have given you the tools to make. The numbers are internally consistent throughout, so you can check any figure against its source. I'd encourage you to do each stage on paper before you read my treatment of it.

## The situation

You have about $3.0 million of capital you can commit to real estate, and the discipline to leave at least 10% of it untouched as a reserve. A broker you've met twice sends you an offering memorandum for **Juniper Gardens**, a 48-unit garden apartment community built in 1986 in Ridgemont, a suburb of a mid-sized metro. It is asking **$6,240,000**, or $130,000 a door.

The seller is a 71-year-old owner who has managed the property himself, with help from his brother, for twenty-two years. He is retiring for health reasons, and the broker says he wants a clean, quick sale. The building is 94% occupied. Interiors are original or lightly updated: oak-veneer cabinets, vinyl flooring, builder-grade fixtures. The owner pays water, sewer, and trash. There is no formal management company.

## The documents on the table

The broker's package contains:

- An **offering memorandum** showing NOI of **$435,160**, a stated cap rate of **6.97%** at the asking price, and a "market rent" of $1,300 against in-place rents averaging $1,125, with the line "$175 a month of upside in every unit."
- A **T-12** that matches the OM's income line.
- A **rent roll** of 48 units.
- A summary of capital improvements the seller claims to have made over the years.

What's *not* in the package, at first: twelve months of bank statements, an insurance quote, the current tax bill with reassessment rules, any property condition report, or a list of service contracts. You'll need to ask for these.

## The market

Ridgemont has a stock of about 5,200 apartment units, with 218 under construction, absorption of about 190 units a year, a vacancy rate of 5.6%, and renter household income of about $52,000. The largest employer, a hospital system, provides 6% of local jobs. Two new buildings downtown are asking $1,360 and offering three weeks of free rent.

## The clock

You expect to get a letter of intent signed within a week, a 30-day diligence period after the purchase agreement, and a closing roughly 60 days after the LOI. Between the LOI and closing, your lender's rate quote is *not* locked. Your plan is a five-year hold.

## What you are being asked to decide

At each stage you face a decision, and the stages are linked:

1. **Stage 1:** Is this a deal at all, and at what price will you bid? What's your walk-away price?
2. **Stage 2:** What did diligence reveal, how do you convert it into a renegotiation, and what do you accept?
3. **Stage 3:** How do you finance and execute the plan? What happens when the financing moves against you? How much can the deal take before it breaks? What's your exit?

## The modules you'll draw on

You'll need the market-reading tools from Module 2, the lease and income-quality thinking of Module 3, the NOI rebuild and cap rate work of Module 4, the coverage ratios of Module 5, the debt choices of Module 6, the return measures and sensitivity grid of Module 7, the diligence process of Module 8, the renovation math of Module 9, the management and operations lessons of Module 10, the tax thinking of Module 11, the stress testing of Module 12, and the exit logic of Module 13. Module 1 is the frame around all of it: four return engines, leverage that cuts both ways, and the idea that you make your money when you buy.

## Ground rules for the model

To keep the arithmetic traceable, the model makes these simplifying assumptions: a five-year hold; base NOI growth of 3% a year; a sale at a 7.0% cap rate (50 basis points above the 6.5% going-in market cap rate) with 3% sale costs; management fee of 4.5% of income; reserves of $250 per unit; all renovation and repair capital funded at closing (which slightly understates the true IRR because in practice some spending comes later); and no income taxes in the returns. Interiors are renovated at natural turnover (19 units in year 1, 19 in year 2, 10 in year 3), with a rent premium of $140 a month on renovated units and a partial utility bill-back. The loan is sized at a 1.25 DSCR on in-place NOI. Treat these as the base case, and watch for what changes them.

## How to use this capstone

Read each stage's setup and write down your own answers first: your bid, your list of diligence findings, your negotiation position. Then read the treatment, compare, and take the quiz. The quiz has seven decisions, mostly with trade-offs rather than a single right answer; the explanation behind each answer matters more than the answer itself.

One last thing. In a real deal, the numbers in the OM are the starting point, never the conclusion. The reason this capstone exists is to put you through the process in which the OM is a claim, diligence is the evidence, and the price is the verdict.

One more point before you begin: nothing in this case is fraudulent or exotic. Every problem you will meet is ordinary, the kind that turns up in most older apartment deals. The skill being tested is not catching a villain; it is staying methodical when a number does not reconcile.

# Stage 1: Screen and Underwrite

## The setup

Before you spend a dollar on diligence, you decide whether Juniper Gardens deserves your attention at all, and at what price. You do three things: read the market, rebuild the income, and test the plan.

## The numbers

**The market scorecard (Module 2):**

| Signal | Juniper Gardens' submarket | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Pipeline as % of existing stock | 218 / 5,200 = 4.2% | Yellow |
| Pipeline in years of absorption | 218 / 190 = 1.1 years | Yellow |
| Rent-to-income (in place) | $1,125 × 12 / $52,000 = 26.0% | Green: room to grow |
| Largest employer's share of jobs | 6% | Yellow |
| Concessions at the newest buildings | 3 weeks free | Yellow: effective rent $1,360 × 11.25 / 12 = $1,275 |

**Comps (adjusted to Juniper Gardens' condition, Module 2):** five unrenovated comps average **$1,160**, so loss-to-lease is only $35 a month. Five renovated comps average **$1,266**, a premium of about **$141** over the in-place $1,125. The OM's $1,300 market rent isn't supported; the renovated premium is.

**The NOI rebuild (Module 4), at a $5,650,000 bid:**

| Line | Seller's OM | Your underwriting |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross potential rent (48 × $1,125 × 12) | $648,000 | $648,000 |
| Vacancy and credit loss | -$19,440 (3%) | -$35,640 (5.5%) |
| Other income | $21,600 | $19,200 |
| **Effective gross income** | **$650,160** | **$631,560** |
| Property taxes | $63,000 | $70,625 (1.25% of price) |
| Insurance | $24,000 | $30,000 |
| Utilities | $52,000 | $54,000 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $52,000 | $52,000 |
| Admin and marketing | $9,600 | $9,600 |
| Landscaping and snow | $14,400 | $14,400 |
| On-site payroll | $0 | $7,200 |
| Management | $0 | $28,420 (4.5%) |
| Reserves ($250 per unit) | $0 | $12,000 |
| **Total expenses** | **$215,000 (33.1%)** | **$278,245 (44.1%)** |
| **NOI** | **$435,160** | **$353,315** |

At the $6,240,000 ask, your NOI is $345,940 (taxes rise with the price), a **5.54%** cap rate against the OM's 6.97%. At a 6.5% cap rate, your NOI supports about **$5.44 million**.

**The business plan (Module 9):** interior renovation of all 48 units at $9,500 each, $60,000 of exterior and common areas, 15% contingency, 3% soft costs: **$608,880** in total. Premium of $140 a month ($80,640 a year at full run rate), plus a utility bill-back recovering 60% of the $54,000 utility bill. Check affordability: ($1,125 + $140 + $56) × 12 / $52,000 = **30.5%**, right at the limit of what tenants can bear. An 80% bill-back would put it at 30.9%.

**Returns (Module 7), loan at 6.75%, sized at 1.25 DSCR:**

| Price | NOI cap rate | Yield on cost | Equity | Levered IRR | Multiple |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $6,240,000 (ask) | 5.54% | 6.89% | $3,643,160 | 6.4% | 1.33x |
| $5,650,000 | 6.25% | 7.64% | $2,965,556 | 11.5% | 1.66x |
| $5,500,000 | 6.46% | 7.85% | $2,793,284 | 13.0% | 1.77x |

## What the veteran sees

The OM's expense ratio of 33.1% was the first thing to notice: a 1986 garden community with owner-paid utilities and no management or payroll shouldn't be that clean. Your rebuilt NOI is 18.8% lower, and the cap rate at the ask falls from 6.97% to 5.54%. At the ask, the deal returns 6.4%, which is below Treasury-plus-a-premium territory, for a project with a renovation to execute. It is not a deal at that price.

The market is workable: supply is moderate, tenants have room today, the employer base is okay. The comps support the premium of $140 but not the broker's $175 or $1,300. And the plan is right at the affordability edge, so the premium is less certain than the spreadsheet suggests.

Your decision: **bid $5,650,000** (9.5% below ask), contingent on diligence, and set your **walk-away at about $5,500,000**, where the base case clears 13%, the bottom of the range I'd want for a value-add with execution risk (Module 7). You expect to find things in diligence that will move the price down, which is why you can bid above your walk-away before you know; any trade you win in Stage 2 lowers what you eventually pay.

## Change one thing

If the renovated premium were $100 rather than $140, yield on cost at $5,650,000 would fall from 7.64% to about 7.3%, and the IRR from 11.5% to roughly 9.3%. A $40 swing, within the error bar of any rent survey, costs more than two points of IRR. Before you commit to all 48 units, you'll renovate four model units and lease them at the target premium. If they don't lease at $135 or more within 30 days, the plan needs to be rethought.

# Stage 2: Diligence and Renegotiation

## The setup

The seller accepts your $5,650,000 LOI. You have a 30-day diligence period. You follow the order from Module 8: financials first, then leases, then physical, then everything else. The seller's brother takes ten days to produce the bank statements, which tells you something before you open them.

## The numbers

**The three-way reconciliation:**

| Source | Annual income |
|---|---:|
| T-12 reported rental income | $610,000 |
| Bank deposits, twelve months | $590,200 |
| **Gap** | **$19,800 (3.2%)** |

| Reconciling the gap | Amount | Recurring? |
|---|---:|---|
| Bad debt written off after being booked as income | $10,800 | Yes |
| Concessions and rent credits booked as full income | $6,300 | Yes |
| December rent deposited in January | $2,300 | No, timing |
| Unexplained | $400 | Treat as yes |
| **Recurring total** | **$17,500** | |

Value at a 6.5% cap rate: $17,500 / 0.065 = **$269,231**.

**The inspection (Module 8):**

| Finding | Cost | Timing |
|---|---:|---|
| Sewer lateral failure | $38,500 | Immediate |
| Parking lot | $46,000 | Immediate |
| Smoke detectors, GFCI outlets | $14,000 | Immediate |
| **Immediate** | **$98,500** | |
| Roof (21,000 sf × $7.50) | $157,500 | Years 1-3 |
| Water heaters (20 × $2,400) | $48,000 | Years 1-3 |
| **Near-term** | **$205,500** | |
| **Total** | **$304,000** | $6,333 per unit |

Your reserve of $250 per unit for three years is $36,000, only 11.8% of what was found.

**The renegotiation ask:** the recurring income gap ($269,231), all immediate repairs ($98,500), and half the roof ($78,750), for a total of **$446,481**, implying a price of about $5,203,519. The seller counters. After a call in which you walk him through the bank statements, you settle at **$5,250,000**, a reduction of **$400,000**, about 90% of your ask. Because the county reassesses at the sale price, the lower price also cuts taxes by 1.25% × $400,000 = $5,000 a year.

## What the veteran sees

Two things happened that would have been invisible if you'd gone straight to the inspection. First, the bank statements showed that $17,500 a year of the seller's income doesn't arrive, and not through fraud: through a bookkeeping style that books rent when due and writes off losses later. Second, the inspection found a building whose seller hadn't been funding capital. Neither is exotic. Both are normal. Both are priced.

The key judgment in this stage is to ask for the items that are documented and large, and let the small ones go. You didn't ask for the water heaters or the other half of the roof; those are the price of owning a 1986 building. You asked for what you could prove, in dollars, and you gave the seller a short, defensible list. That's why he settled instead of resisting.

Notice what the new price does to your return. At the original $5,650,000 with the diligence findings unaddressed, the model would return only **9.4%**. At $5,250,000, with the same findings, it returns **13.4%** (at the 6.75% rate you were quoted at the time). The renegotiation is what makes the deal work.

## Change one thing

Suppose the seller refuses to move on the roof and the bank-statement gap, and counters at $5,500,000, your original walk-away price. With the diligence findings still valid, the model at $5,500,000 returns only **10.8%**, not 13.0%. The price at which the base case again clears 13% is about **$5,290,000**. Your walk-away has moved down by about $210,000, because the income was $17,500 lower than you believed and you carry $304,000 of deferred needs. A walk-away is a function of what you know, and what you know just changed.

# Stage 3: Finance, Execute, and Exit

## The setup

You're under contract at **$5,250,000**. Two things now matter: the financing, which hasn't been locked, and the plan's execution. Between the LOI and closing, your lender's quote moves from 6.75% to **7.25%**. Because the loan is sized at a 1.25 DSCR, a higher rate means a smaller loan.

## The numbers

**The rate shock (Module 5):**

| | At 6.75% | At 7.25% |
|---|---:|---:|
| In-place NOI (after the $17,500 gap) | $340,815 | $340,815 |
| Maximum loan at 1.25 DSCR | $3,503,092 | $3,330,662 |
| Debt service | $272,652 | $272,652 |
| Equity required | $2,686,038 | $2,858,468 |
| Levered IRR (base case) | 13.4% | **12.6%** |

The half-point rise costs **$172,430** of extra equity and 0.8 points of IRR. To restore 13.4% at 7.25% you'd need the price to be about $76,000 lower, which is not something the seller owes you.

**The debt choice (Module 6):**

| | A: 7-year fixed | B: 3-year floating bridge |
|---|---|---|
| Loan | $3,330,662 | $3,600,000 interest-only |
| Rate | 7.25% fixed | 7.8% (index 4.3% + 3.5%) |
| Annual cost | $272,652 | $280,800 |
| DSCR on in-place NOI | 1.25 | 1.21 |
| Rate cap | Not needed | About $45,000 |
| Prepayment | 5-4-3-2-1-1-open; year-5 sale costs 1%, about $31,434 | Typically low |
| Risk at maturity | None before year 7 | Refinance in year 3 |

If renovations slip and year-3 NOI is only about $361,570 (in-place plus growth), a lender at 8.25% would lend roughly $3,208,543 against the bridge balance of $3,600,000: a gap of **$391,457**. You choose **Option A**: the plan stabilizes in year 4, and a loan that matures in year 3 puts the refinance before the plan is finished.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $371,528 | $272,652 | $98,876 | 1.36 |

Less 3% costs ($222,962) and the loan balance ($3,143,438), net proceeds are **$4,065,673**. Total profit $2,108,540; equity multiple **1.74x**; levered IRR **12.6%**; yield on cost 7.9%.

| Premium | Exit 6.5% | Exit 7.0% | Exit 7.5% |

| $100 | 12.9% (1.77x) | 10.4% (1.58x) | 7.9% (1.42x) |

**The stress test on in-place NOI (Module 12):**

| Scenario | NOI | DSCR | Annual cash flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Base | $340,815 | 1.25 | +$68,163 |
| Vacancy +5 points | $308,415 | 1.13 | +$35,763 |

| Expenses +10% | $313,490 | 1.15 | +$40,838 |
| Combined: vacancy +4, rents -3%, expenses +8% | $275,442 | 1.01 | +$2,790 |
| Severe: vacancy +6, rents -5%, expenses +12% | $240,471 | 0.88 | -$32,180 |

Breakeven occupancy at in-place expenses and debt service is 81.8%. Combining a $100 premium, a 25% renovation cost overrun, a 7.5% exit cap, and the 7.25% rate, the IRR is **7.5%** (1.40x).

## What the veteran sees

At 12.6% and 1.74x, this deal sits at the low end of what a value-add should return, with a thin cushion against the premium and exit assumptions: the grid shows IRRs from 7.9% to 16.6% depending on two inputs. The loan is sound: even the combined downside keeps DSCR at 1.01 and cash flow positive, and only the severe case is negative, costing $32,180 a year, or $64,360 over two years. A reserve of roughly **$100,000** (the severe two-year shortfall plus a cushion) in addition to your renovation contingency would let you survive that case without selling.

On the taxes (Module 11): you aren't a real estate professional, so depreciation losses would be passive and suspended. A cost segregation study would add little to your near-term taxes and would add recapture at sale. You skip it for now and revisit it after speaking with your CPA about your income and status.

On the exit (Module 13): at year 5, you compare the marginal return of holding with what the equity could earn elsewhere, you check whether the loan's 1% step-down fee ($31,434) tips the decision, and you decide whether to sell, refinance, or hold. The decision rule is written before you start.

Would I sign? Yes, provided that the four model units lease at or near $140 before the rest of the program is committed, the reserve is funded, and the walk-away is firm. The deal does not pay for mistakes; it pays for discipline.

## Change one thing

Say the four model units lease at only $105. Re-run the grid: at a $100 premium and a 7.0% exit, the IRR is 10.4%. You now choose among three paths: renovate less (a lighter scope costing perhaps $6,500 a unit, with a smaller premium), renovate only the best-located units, or hold the building as a stabilized asset and skip the renovation. The honest answer depends on a calculation you can now do: yield on cost for each scope against the 6.5% to 7.0% you'd get from just owning the building. A value-add plan that fails its model-unit test isn't a failure; it's the cheapest piece of information in the whole deal.

# Quiz

## Q1 | scenario

**Scenario.** Juniper Gardens' OM shows NOI of $435,160 and a 6.97% cap rate at the $6,240,000 ask. The OM has no management fee, no payroll, and no reserves, and its expense ratio is 33%. Your rebuilt NOI at the ask is $345,940.

What does the ask really imply, and how should you respond?

- [ ] A 6.97% cap rate, which is fair for the market, so bid near the ask and negotiate on details later :: The 6.97% depends on an NOI that omits management, payroll, and reserves. It is a marketing number, not a measure of the property's earning power.
- [x] About 5.5% on your NOI, which supports roughly $5.3 million at 6.5%. :: $345,940 / $6,240,000 = 5.54%. At 6.5% the same NOI supports about $5.32 million, so the ask is about $900,000 too high on income alone.
- [ ] About 7.5%, since a lower NOI always means a higher cap rate :: A lower NOI at the same price lowers the cap rate (NOI divided by price), it doesn't raise it.
- [ ] You cannot value the building until you have seen the inspection report and the environmental findings :: Income can be rebuilt first, cheaply, and should be. The inspection answers a different question and comes later, after the numbers have survived.

> Rebuild the number before you argue about the price. A cap rate on the wrong NOI is just a way of repeating the broker's story.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** Your plan adds a $140 rent premium and a bill-back of $56 a month on top of the current $1,125 rent. Renter household income is $52,000. Rent-to-income is 26.0% today and would be 30.5% after the plan.

How should this change the way you execute the plan?

- [ ] Proceed at full speed with all 48 units, since 30.5% is close enough to the 30% rule of thumb :: Being at the ceiling means there is no room to absorb surprises. Moving all 48 units at once leaves no way to correct if tenants resist.
- [ ] Cancel the bill-back entirely and double the rent premium instead to make up the difference :: A bigger premium faces the same affordability ceiling and the comps do not support it. It makes the problem worse.
- [x] Test four model units, phase the bill-back, and model a $100 case. :: At the affordability edge, evidence beats optimism. Model units show the real premium, renewals soften the bill-back, and a $100 case shows what happens if you're wrong.
- [ ] Ignore affordability because tenants have accepted increases before :: Past acceptance came from a lower starting point. The ceiling is an input to every new increase, especially stacked ones.

> When the market's ceiling is in view, prove the premium before you spend on all the units.

## Q3 | scenario

**Scenario.** The seller's T-12 reports $610,000 of rental income but bank deposits total $590,200. The $19,800 gap breaks down as $10,800 of bad debt, $6,300 of concessions, $2,300 of December rent deposited in January, and $400 unexplained. You value at a 6.5% cap rate.

How much value does the gap represent, and what is the right treatment?

- [ ] About $305,000, by capitalizing the whole $19,800 gap :: This counts the $2,300 timing item, which won't recur. Capitalizing a one-time item overstates the loss.
- [ ] Nothing, because the T-12 is the statement that the seller certified and delivered to you :: The T-12 is a claim. Deposits show what actually arrived, and the difference reduces what a buyer will collect.
- [x] About $269,000, from the $17,500 that recurs. :: Bad debt, concessions, and the unexplained $400 are treated as recurring. $17,500 / 0.065 = $269,231, and the one-time $2,300 is excluded.
- [ ] About $35,000, since a measured loss of this kind should be split evenly with the seller :: There is no basis for splitting a measured recurring loss. It's evidence you present, not an amount to be halved by default.

> You pay for income collected. Separate the leaks that recur from the timing items that don't.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** After inspections, you ask for a price reduction that reflects $269,231 of income gap, $98,500 of immediate repairs, and half the roof ($78,750), totaling $446,481. The seller says, "Instead of cutting the price, I'll fix the sewer, the parking lot, and the safety items myself before closing."

What is the best response?

- [ ] Accept, since the seller doing the repairs himself removes the need to negotiate over cost :: A seller managing his own repairs has a reason to do them cheaply. Without standards you inherit the risk of poor work.
- [ ] Reject all repairs by the seller and demand the entire $446,481 :: Insisting on the full amount and refusing any cooperation risks the deal over what is a negotiable part. The strong ask is the documented one, and settling near it is a good outcome.
- [x] Prefer a credit; if repairs, require permits and a re-inspection. :: Cash lets you control the work. If he insists on doing it, verified quality and a warranty protect you. The income gap isn't a repair at all and still needs a price adjustment.
- [ ] Agree and drop the income gap from the negotiation to keep goodwill :: The income gap is the largest item at $269,231, and it is not a repair issue. Dropping it for goodwill gives away most of the value you found.

> Take cash when you can. If you take work, verify it. And don't trade the biggest item for the easiest one.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** Between the LOI and closing, your lender's quote rises from 6.75% to 7.25%. The loan shrinks by $172,430, you must add that much equity, and the base-case IRR falls from 13.4% to 12.6%. The seller is not responsible for rate moves. The model-unit test is not yet complete.

What is the soundest response?

- [ ] Demand that the seller cut the price by $172,430 to offset the lower loan :: Interest rates are a market condition, not the seller's responsibility. This demand would likely damage the deal.
- [ ] Walk away, since the IRR is now below the 13% hurdle :: A 0.4-point miss is not obviously a reason to walk. The better question is how much the model-unit evidence and the grid change your view of the base case.
- [x] Re-run the grid, finish the model-unit test, and decide. :: The deal is now 12.6%, with a range of roughly 8% to 17% depending on the premium and the exit. Evidence on the premium is what decides whether to proceed. A modest ask is fine; expecting a rate-driven credit isn't.
- [ ] Add a high-interest second loan to restore the original equity check and keep the same cash in :: This raises debt service, lowers coverage below your threshold, and swaps a known equity cost for a riskier one.

> A change in terms is a reason to re-underwrite, not to blame someone or to hope. Run the grid and decide from the evidence.

## Q6 | scenario

**Scenario.** On in-place NOI, the combined downside (vacancy +4 points, rents -3%, expenses +8%) leaves DSCR at 1.01 and cash flow at +$2,790. The severe case (vacancy +6, rents -5%, expenses +12%) gives a DSCR of 0.88 and a cash flow of -$32,180 a year. You have $3.0 million of capital, $2.86 million of which is committed to equity.

What reserve policy is most sensible?

- [ ] Rely on the renovation contingency, since it already covers surprises :: Contingency is for construction costs. A cash shortfall from operations is a different risk that it won't be there to cover.
- [x] Hold about $100,000 in liquid reserves beyond the renovation budget. :: The severe case costs about $64,360 over two years. A reserve near $100,000 covers that with a cushion and keeps a bad two years from forcing a sale.
- [ ] Hold no reserve, since the combined case is still cash-flow positive :: The combined case leaves almost no cushion, and the severe case is negative. Skipping the reserve means depending on conditions staying better than your stress test.
- [ ] Use the full $3.0 million as equity to lower the loan size :: Spending all your capital leaves nothing to absorb a bad year. A smaller loan helps, but without liquidity the first shortfall becomes a crisis.

> Reserves are what turn a stressful year into a survivable one. Set the number from your severe case, not from your hopes.

## Q7 | scenario

**Scenario.** You can finance with a 7-year fixed loan at 7.25% ($272,652 a year) or a 3-year floating bridge loan at 7.8% interest-only ($280,800 a year, plus a $45,000 rate cap).
