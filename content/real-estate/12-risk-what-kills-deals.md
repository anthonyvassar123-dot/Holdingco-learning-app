# Lesson

Over fifty years I have watched a lot of deals fail, and almost none of them failed because of one thing. Nobody lost a building because occupancy dropped four points. They lost it because occupancy dropped four points, and insurance renewed 40% higher, and the loan matured in a market where lenders wanted lower leverage, and the partners had no reserve, and the one person who could have written a check was in the middle of a divorce. Deals don't die of a single cause. They die of a chain.

That's the idea this module is built on: risk isn't an event, it's an accumulation. Your job as an owner is to understand what each link in the chain is, how much room you have before each one breaks, and to decide in advance what you'll do, so the decision is made by the calm version of you and not the panicked one.

## The risks, sorted by how often they hurt

Not all risks are equal. In my experience, these are the ones that actually take buildings away from owners, roughly in order of frequency:

**1. Financing risk.** The loan matures, or resets, or covenants trip, at a bad time. This is the most common killer of otherwise sound properties. A building that earns exactly what you projected can still be lost if the loan can't be refinanced at the same size.

**2. Operating cost shocks.** Insurance, property taxes, and utilities. These are outside your control and have repriced by 20% to 100% in some markets within a year. Because they sit in expenses and expenses aren't growing at your assumed 3%, they arrive as unpleasant surprises.

**3. Income shortfalls.** Vacancy, concessions, bad debt, and a single big tenant leaving. The impact depends on your breakeven occupancy from Module 5.

**4. Capital surprises.** A roof, a sewer, a boiler, a foundation. Anything not in the reserves.

**5. Execution risk.** On value-add projects: cost overruns, delays, contractors who disappear, premiums that don't appear.

**6. Partner and people risk.** A partner who can't fund a capital call, a manager who steals, a key person who leaves, a disagreement with no process for resolving it.

**7. Market and regulatory risk.** New supply, a major employer leaving, rent regulation, changes in tax treatment, or a natural disaster.

Notice that the two biggest items are financial structure and costs you don't control. Neither is about whether the building is good. That's why a well-run building can still fail.

## Stress testing: the discipline

A stress test asks, "What happens if several things go moderately wrong at once?" The key phrase is *at once*. Single-variable sensitivities (rents down 5%) are reassuring because they rarely break a sound deal. It's the combinations that do, and the combinations are more likely than you think because many risks share a cause. A recession raises vacancy, lowers rents, and tightens credit at the same time. A hurricane season raises insurance, causes a casualty, and strains your manager at the same time.

Build a set of scenarios:

- **Base case:** your underwriting.
- **Single shocks:** occupancy down 5 points, rents down 5%, expenses up 10%, one at a time.
- **Combined downside:** a moderate version of all three together (for example, vacancy up 4 points, rents down 3%, expenses up 8%).
- **Severe:** a harder version (vacancy up 6, rents down 5%, expenses up 12%).
- **Financing stress:** rates 150 basis points higher at refinance, NOI 10% below plan.

For each, compute NOI, DSCR, and monthly cash surplus or shortfall. The shortfall times the number of months it might last is the **reserve you need**. Example 1 does this for Cedar Ridge and shows how a loan that survives every single shock can still fail the combined case.

## The insurance and tax shocks

For most of the 2010s, you could underwrite insurance and taxes with a growth rate and a shrug. That stopped being safe in the 2020s. In some regions, insurance renewals came in 30% to 100% higher, with higher deductibles and reduced coverage. Property taxes can reset sharply on sale or after a reassessment cycle, and in some places they're passed to renters in ways that matter for rent-to-income.

Treat these as their own stress variables. When underwriting: get a written insurance quote from an agent who writes in that market, not an estimate. Ask what the deductible is (many policies now carry wind or hail deductibles of 1% to 5% of the insured value, which on a $5 million building is $50,000 to $250,000 out of your pocket per event). Ask whether flood is covered (it usually isn't) and whether the policy pays replacement cost or actual cash value. For taxes, find out the reassessment rules and appeal rights, and model the tax bill at the *purchase price*, not the seller's.

## The refinance gap

Most commercial loans are not fully amortizing. A five- or seven-year loan leaves a balloon due at maturity, and the loan that replaces it is sized on the property's NOI and the rate and lending standards *at that moment*. If NOI is lower or rates are higher or lenders are tighter than when you borrowed, the new loan is smaller than the old one, and you must make up the difference in cash. That difference is the **refinance gap**, and it's the mechanism behind most distressed sales in rate-shock periods. Example 3 quantifies it.

The way to defend against it is not to hope. It's to take longer loan terms where you can, to size the original loan conservatively, to pay down principal when cash allows, to keep reserves for a paydown, and to start refinancing early, six to twelve months before maturity, not three. If you see a gap forming, tell your lender and your partners early. Lenders deal with problems more flexibly when they hear about them from you.

## Capital calls and people

A **capital call** is a demand for more equity from the owners. If you're alone, it's your problem. If you have partners, it's a governance problem, and many partnerships have no answer written down. Before you invest with anyone, ask: What happens if someone can't fund? Is there a dilution formula, a loan from the other partners at a punitive rate, a forced sale? Are the consequences clear enough that a reasonable person won't be shocked? A partnership agreement is the cheapest insurance in real estate, and the one most often skipped.

The people risk also includes your manager (Module 10), your broker, your lender, and, importantly, you. Key-person risk is real in small deals: if you are the only person who knows how the building runs, what happens if you're unavailable for three months?

## Setting rules in advance

I keep three kinds of rules written down before I close on a deal, so I don't improvise when stressed:

1. **A reserve policy.** For example: 6 months of debt service and operating expenses in liquid reserves, plus a capital reserve of $300 per unit per year.
2. **Trigger points.** If DSCR falls below 1.10 for two consecutive months, I do X (cut discretionary spending, call the lender). If occupancy falls below breakeven for a quarter, I do Y.
3. **A walk-away rule.** The maximum additional capital I'm willing to put into a property before I consider selling or handing it back. Deciding the number in advance keeps you from throwing good money after bad.

## Run a pre-mortem

Before you close, do the exercise I've done on every deal for decades: imagine it is five years from now, the deal has failed, and you're explaining why to a friend. Write down the three most likely reasons. It sounds morbid, but it works, because it frees you from defending the deal and lets you look at it from the outside. The reasons that surface are usually concrete and fixable: "We were floating and the cap expired," "The anchor tenant left and we had no reserve," "The manager was too small to handle a renovation." Each one points to a decision you can make today: buy a longer cap, hold a larger reserve, hire a better manager. If the failure stories all sound unfixable, that's your answer about the deal.

## Concentration and disaster

Two last sources of risk are worth a line each. **Concentration** means too much riding on one thing: one tenant (40% of a center's rent), one employer (11% of local jobs), one lender (all your loans maturing in one year), one market (all your buildings in one metro). Diversification isn't free for a small owner, so the practical rule is to know your largest concentration and size your reserves to it. **Disaster** means casualty: fire, flood, wind, earthquake. Insurance addresses most of it, but only if the coverage is right: replacement cost rather than depreciated value, adequate limits, and coverage for lost rent during rebuilding (usually 12 months). If your building burned tomorrow, could you rebuild under today's codes with the proceeds, and would your loan survive the 12 to 18 months of no income? Ask your agent that question in those words.

## The mistakes I see most

**Testing one thing at a time.** Real downturns hit several variables together.

**Treating the lender's DSCR as a safety margin.** The lender's covenant protects the lender. Your safety margin is the stress case.

**Underwriting insurance at last year's premium.** The market moves; quotes are cheap and necessary.

**Ignoring the maturity.** The loan that is fine today can be the problem in year five.

**No reserves, or reserves you'd spend on opportunities.** A reserve that is used for the next deal isn't a reserve.

**Partnerships without a written answer to "what if someone can't fund?"** The time to answer is before the call.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**How much cushion is enough?** I'd rather own a smaller building I could hold through a bad two years than a larger one I couldn't. The cost of caution is a little lower return in good years. The cost of its absence is the building. A useful rule: the deal should still cover debt service in the combined downside case, with cash reserves to cover any shortfall for a year.

**When to give the keys back.** Occasionally the right decision is to stop feeding a property. If the value is below the loan, the cash flow is negative, and the cure requires more capital than you'd rationally commit, a structured exit (a sale, a deed in lieu, or a negotiated workout) can be better than burning your reserves. If your loan is non-recourse, this is a business decision. If you've given a guarantee, it's far more serious, and you should involve an attorney early.

**Insure for the real risks.** Review coverage annually with an agent who understands your property type. A cheaper policy that excludes the event that actually happens is not a saving.

## If you remember nothing else

- Deals die of chains, not single events. Stress-test combinations, not just one variable at a time.
- Insurance, taxes, and the balloon are the three risks you can't control; model them with real quotes and a refinance-gap calculation.
- Write your reserve policy, triggers, and walk-away rule down before you close.

# Example 1: The Stress Table

## The setup

Cedar Ridge (40 units): gross potential rent $696,000, other income $18,000, 6% vacancy, operating expenses $334,240, NOI $338,000, debt service $270,400. You run five scenarios.

## The numbers

| Scenario | NOI | DSCR | Annual cash flow | Monthly |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Base case | $338,000 | 1.25 | +$67,600 | +$5,633 |
| Vacancy +5 points (to 11%) | $303,200 | 1.12 | +$32,800 | +$2,733 |
| Rents down 5% | $305,288 | 1.13 | +$34,888 | +$2,907 |
| Expenses up 10% | $304,576 | 1.13 | +$34,176 | +$2,848 |
| **Combined:** vacancy 10%, rents -3%, expenses +8% | **$264,629** | **0.98** | **-$5,771** | **-$481** |
| **Severe:** vacancy 12%, rents -5%, expenses +12% | **$225,507** | **0.83** | **-$44,893** | **-$3,741** |

## What the veteran sees

Every single shock leaves the building cash-flowing: DSCR of 1.12 to 1.13, thin but positive. If that were the only test, you'd say the loan was fine. The combined case flips it: DSCR below 1.0 and a shortfall of $481 a month. The severe case produces a $44,893 annual shortfall.

What does that mean for reserves? Suppose the combined downside lasts two years: you need about $11,500. The severe case over two years: about $89,800. Against $1.83 million of equity, those aren't catastrophic numbers. A reserve of $90,000 survives the severe case for two years. Without it, a $3,741 monthly shortfall can kill the deal in a year. The building is fine; it is the reserve that decides.

Another view: the loan was sized to exactly 1.25. A loan sized at 1.40 (about $3.10 million rather than $3.47 million) would carry debt service of $241,500; in the combined case NOI of $264,629 would still produce a DSCR of 1.10 and a surplus. That is what a margin of safety is.

## Change one thing

Add 40% to the insurance line ($24,000 → $33,600, an extra $9,600) on top of the combined case. NOI falls to $255,029; DSCR to 0.94; the shortfall to $15,371 a year. Insurance alone moved the outcome by $9,600, which is more than the shortfall we started with. That is why you get a quote, and why it is the first number I check, not the last.

# Example 2: The Shocks You Don't Control

## The setup

A 24-unit building with a rebuilt NOI of $178,359 (from Module 4), $21,000 of insurance, and $48,000 of property taxes. You model what happens if insurance reprices by 45% at renewal and a reassessment raises taxes 20%.

## The numbers

| Item | Before | After | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Insurance | $21,000 | $30,450 | +$9,450 |
| Property taxes | $48,000 | $57,600 | +$9,600 |
| **Total** | | | **+$19,050** |
| NOI | $178,359 | $159,309 | -10.7% |
| Value at a 6.5% cap | $2,743,985 | $2,450,908 | -$293,077 |
| DSCR (loan sized at 1.25 on the old NOI) | 1.25 | 1.12 | |

## What the veteran sees

Two lines that make up 38% of operating expenses moved by $19,050. NOI fell by 10.7%, the value by $293,077, and the DSCR from 1.25 to 1.12. No tenant left, no rent fell, and no one did anything wrong. This is what happens when outside costs reprice faster than your rents can, and it is why I treat insurance and taxes as stress variables of their own.

Rent growth normally offsets cost growth, but only slowly and only if the market allows it. Making up $19,050 would take rent growth of about 5% of income, and two years at the usual 2% to 3% only gets you partway there. And the first lever, raising rent, is bounded by Module 2's affordability ceiling.

## Change one thing

You find that your policy has a 3% wind and hail deductible on a $5.0 million insured value: $150,000 per event. Your annual insurance cost is $30,450 but one storm could cost you $150,000 out of pocket. Compare two policies: one with a 1% deductible ($50,000) at $39,000 a year, and one with a 3% deductible at $30,450. The difference in premium is $8,550 a year; the difference in event exposure is $100,000. If you expect such an event once every twelve years, the cheaper policy costs the same on average, but the cheaper one requires you to hold $150,000 in liquid reserves. Pick the deductible you can fund.

# Example 3: The Refinance Gap

## The setup

You borrowed $3,474,160 at 6.75% on a 5-year term (30-year amortization) against Cedar Ridge. After five years the balance is **$3,261,392**. You need to refinance. The new lender will lend the *lesser* of (a) 1.25 DSCR at an 8.0% rate on a 30-year amortization (constant 8.805%) and (b) 70% of value at a 7.5% cap rate.

## The numbers

| Scenario at maturity | NOI | DSCR-based loan | LTV-based loan | Maximum new loan | Gap vs. balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| NOI grew 3% a year | $380,422 | $3,456,349 | $3,550,605 | $3,456,349 | +$194,957 spare |
| NOI flat | $338,000 | $3,070,921 | $3,154,667 | $3,070,921 | **-$190,471** |
| NOI 10% below today | $304,200 | $2,763,829 | $2,839,200 | $2,763,829 | **-$497,563** |
| NOI 10% below, rate 8.5% | $304,200 | $2,637,488 | $2,839,200 | $2,637,488 | **-$623,904** |

## What the veteran sees

In the base case the refinance works, and you could even take out $195,000. If NOI is flat, you owe $190,000 more than the new lender will provide. If NOI is down 10% and rates are half a point higher, the gap is $624,000, on a property that still has positive cash flow. That is the pattern in every rate-shock cycle: a property that is "fine" at today's income, a lender who isn't, and an owner who needs a check.

The gap is large relative to the $1.83 million of equity you put in (10% to 34%), and large relative to any reasonable reserve. It's why the first question on any loan should be "what's the maturity, and what happens at the maturity?"

## Change one thing

You take a 10-year loan instead of 5-year. The balance after ten years is lower, about $2.97 million (you've paid down principal for longer), and you have five more years of NOI growth to meet the lender's test. Even with flat NOI the gap closes: $338,000 supports $3.07 million, which is above the balance. Longer terms cost a slightly higher rate or less flexibility on prepayment, but they push the test date out and turn a gap into a margin. Match the term to the time you need the cushion.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

Each of the following alone leaves a building cash-flow positive: vacancy up 5 points, rents down 5%, expenses up 10%. Why should you still test the three together?

- [ ] Because lenders require a combined test in every case :: Lenders often run their own scenarios, but your reason is your own survival, not a requirement. You would do the test even if no lender asked.
- [x] Because real downturns tend to hit these variables together, which can break the building. :: Recessions raise vacancy, depress rents, and push costs up at the same time. In the Cedar Ridge example, the combination turned a positive cash flow into a $5,771 shortfall.
- [ ] Because combined tests always give a lower value than single tests :: Combined scenarios do produce larger shocks, but that's the result, not the purpose. The purpose is to see where the structure breaks.
- [ ] Because single-variable tests are not accurate :: Each single-variable test is accurate for its question. They just ask a milder question than the one that kills deals.

> Deals fail in chains. Test the chain, not the links one by one.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** A 24-unit building's NOI is $178,359. Insurance is quoted to rise 45% (from $21,000) and property taxes are likely to be reassessed upward by 20% (from $48,000). The loan was sized to 1.25 DSCR on the old NOI.

Roughly what happens to NOI and coverage?

- [ ] NOI falls about 1%, and coverage is essentially unchanged :: The combined increase is $19,050, which is about 10.7% of NOI, not 1%.
- [ ] NOI falls about 5%, and coverage drops to about 1.19 :: A 5% fall would be about $8,900. The actual change from these two lines is more than twice that.
- [x] NOI falls about 11%, and coverage drops to about 1.12. :: Insurance rises $9,450 and taxes $9,600, a total of $19,050. NOI falls to $159,309 and DSCR to about 1.12.
- [ ] NOI falls about 25%, and the loan goes into default :: The drop is large but not that large. The deal still covers its debt at 1.12, though with far less cushion.

> Costs you can't control reprice faster than rents. Model them with real quotes, not growth rates.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

You borrowed against a property on a 5-year term. At maturity, NOI is flat and rates have risen. What determines how much the new lender will give you?

- [ ] The balance of your existing loan :: Your existing balance is what you owe, not what a new lender will provide. Lenders size new loans on the property's income and value.
- [ ] The original appraisal at the time of purchase :: An old appraisal is irrelevant. A new lender uses current NOI, current rates, and current value.
- [x] Current NOI, rates, and lending standards at that moment :: The new lender applies DSCR, LTV, and debt-yield tests to today's numbers. If they produce a smaller loan than your balance, you must fill the gap.
- [ ] The purchase price you paid five years earlier :: Price paid is history. The new loan reflects what the property is worth and earns now.

> The loan you can get tomorrow depends on tomorrow's market, not on the loan you got yesterday.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** You and two partners bought a building. The partnership agreement says nothing about what happens if someone cannot fund a capital call. The property now needs a $150,000 paydown at refinance, and one partner, who owns 30%, says he can only fund half his share.

What is the most important lesson here?

- [ ] Partners should never invest together on real estate deals :: Many partnerships work well. The problem is the missing agreement, not the idea of partnering.
- [ ] The two other partners should simply cover the shortfall and say nothing :: This sets a precedent, gives the third partner no incentive to change, and leaves the partnership unresolved the next time.
- [x] The agreement needed a rule for unfunded calls before anyone invested. :: Dilution, partner loans, or a forced sale can each be reasonable, but all need to be written in advance when everyone is calm and no one is in trouble.
- [ ] Capital calls are unusual and rarely happen in real estate :: They are common when refinances come up short or repairs exceed reserves. That's why the rule has to exist.

> Decide how to handle trouble before the trouble. Written agreements are cheapest when nothing is wrong.

## Q5 | multiple_choice

You are choosing between a policy with a 3% wind and hail deductible at $30,450 a year and one with a 1% deductible at $39,000. On a $5 million insured value, which statement is most accurate?

- [ ] The cheaper policy is always better because it costs $8,550 less each year :: It's cheaper in premium but leaves you exposed to a $150,000 deductible. The savings only matter if you can hold the reserve.
- [x] The cheaper policy shifts $100,000 of event risk to you, so you must be able to fund it. :: The deductible difference is $150,000 versus $50,000. The lower premium is only a saving if you hold liquid reserves to cover a storm.
- [ ] The higher-premium policy is always better because deductibles do not matter :: Deductibles matter a great deal. The choice depends on your reserves, not on a universal rule.
- [ ] Deductibles apply only to the first claim of each year :: Deductibles apply per event, and a wind and hail deductible is a percentage of the insured value, which is why it can be so large.

> Insurance is a trade between premium and the risk you hold yourself. Only hold what you can fund.
