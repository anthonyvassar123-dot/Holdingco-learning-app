# Lesson

A client of a friend of mine, a surgeon with a high salary, bought a $3 million apartment building in a year when he was told that "the depreciation will wipe out your taxes." He hired a firm to do a cost segregation study, took a big first-year paper loss, and told his accountant to use it. His accountant told him, politely, that he couldn't. The surgeon earned too much, did not qualify as a real estate professional, and the loss was passive. It would be carried forward and sit on the books until he sold the property. He had paid for a study that bought him nothing for eleven years, and he'd based the purchase partly on a tax benefit he couldn't use.

Taxes can add or subtract several points of return from a real estate investment. They deserve respect, and they deserve a professional. But they should never be the reason you buy. In this module I want you to understand the mechanics well enough to ask your CPA good questions, and to see why the best tax strategy is usually a good investment held well.

*A caution before we start: tax law changes often, differs by state, and depends on your personal facts. Everything here is educational and simplified. Rates and rules are those I'm aware of as I write this; confirm current law and your specific situation with a CPA who works with real estate investors before acting.*

## Cash flow is not taxable income

Here's the surprise for new owners: the amount of tax you pay on a property has little to do with the cash it produces. Taxable income from a rental is roughly:

*Rental income - operating expenses - mortgage interest - depreciation = taxable income*

Notice what's missing and what's added. Principal payments on your loan are not deductible (they're repayment of debt, not an expense), so they're in your cash flow but not in your taxable income. **Depreciation** is deductible even though no cash leaves your account. Together, these two items drive a gap between taxable income and cash flow that can be large and, in the early years, in your favor.

## Depreciation: the engine

The tax code lets you deduct the cost of a building over its "useful life" because the building is assumed to wear out. For residential rental property, the life is **27.5 years**; for commercial property, **39 years**. Land is not depreciable, so you must allocate your purchase price between land and building, usually with the help of the county's assessed-value ratio or an appraisal. At Maple Court (our $1,600,000 example), allocating 80% to the building gives a depreciable basis of $1,280,000. Divided by 27.5, that's **$46,545 a year** of deductions.

In year one, Maple Court produced $104,000 of NOI, paid $69,861 of interest, and had $46,545 of depreciation. Taxable income: $104,000 - $69,861 - $46,545 = **-$12,407**. The owner collected $23,055 of cash and showed a tax loss. That is the classic "tax-free cash flow."

It is not, however, free money, and the IRS gets some back later. When you sell, depreciation you've claimed (or were entitled to claim) lowers your tax basis, which raises your taxable gain. This is **depreciation recapture**, which we'll price in Example 3. Depreciation defers tax; it does not erase it. Deferral is still valuable, because a dollar of tax paid in ten years is worth less than a dollar paid now, and because there are strategies for deferring it again.

## Active, passive, and professional: whose losses can you use?

A rental loss is usually **passive**, and passive losses can generally offset only passive income, not your wages or business income. Unused passive losses are suspended and carried forward, and released when you sell the property or generate passive income. There are three important exceptions:

- **The small-landlord allowance.** If you "actively participate" (you make management decisions like approving tenants and setting rents), you can deduct up to $25,000 of rental losses against other income. It phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income. Above $150,000, it's gone.
- **Real estate professional status (REPS).** If you spend more than 750 hours a year in real property trades or businesses *and* more than half of your total working time is in real estate, and you materially participate in your rentals, your losses are no longer passive. This is a high bar and a frequent audit target. A full-time job elsewhere usually rules it out. A spouse can qualify for the couple in some cases.
- **Short-term rentals** can sometimes be non-passive if the average stay is short and you materially participate, with different rules from long-term rentals. Details matter, so talk to a CPA.

The upshot is that whether depreciation helps you now or later depends on your income and how you spend your time. For a surgeon with $450,000 of income and no REPS, the suspended loss is a deferred benefit. For a full-time real estate investor, it can offset other income immediately.

## Cost segregation and bonus depreciation

A standard depreciation schedule treats the whole building as one 27.5-year asset. But parts of a building, such as carpet, appliances, some fixtures, parking lots, landscaping, and certain site improvements, wear out faster and qualify for 5-, 7-, or 15-year lives. An engineering-based **cost segregation study** identifies these components and reclassifies them. For apartment buildings, the share reclassified is commonly 15% to 30% of the depreciable basis. Studies typically cost $5,000 to $20,000.

The reclassified components are also eligible for **bonus depreciation**, which lets you deduct a large percentage of their cost in the first year. Bonus depreciation had been phasing down under prior law (80% in 2023, 60% in 2024, 40% in 2025). Legislation enacted in 2025 restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after mid-January 2025, as I understand it, but rules like this are exactly what changes, so confirm the current treatment and your property's placed-in-service date with your CPA.

The result: a large deduction in year one. Example 2 shows that a $5.2 million building can generate more than $880,000 of extra first-year deductions. The usable benefit is large for someone who can use the loss against other income, and it's merely deferred for someone who can't. The cost: the accelerated depreciation on the personal-property components is "recaptured" as ordinary income on sale, at higher rates than the real property portion.

## Selling: recapture, capital gains, and the 1031 exchange

When you sell a property at a gain, there are three layers of taxable gain:

1. **Depreciation recapture on the building ("unrecaptured Section 1250 gain")**: taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%.
2. **The remaining long-term capital gain** (appreciation above your original cost): federal rates of 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income.
3. **The net investment income tax** of 3.8% for higher-income taxpayers, plus **state taxes** where applicable.

A **1031 exchange** lets you defer all of these by exchanging one investment property for another "like-kind" real property. The rules are strict:

- You must use a **qualified intermediary**, who holds the sale proceeds. If you touch the money, the exchange fails.
- You have **45 days** from closing on the sale to *identify* replacement properties in writing, and **180 days** (or the due date of your tax return, if earlier) to close.
- To defer all the gain, you must buy replacement property of equal or greater value, reinvest all the net equity, and replace the debt you paid off. Cash you take out, or debt relief you don't replace, is "boot" and is taxable.
- The new property takes over your old basis, so the deferred tax follows you. You can repeat the exchange indefinitely, and if you hold the property until death, heirs generally receive a "stepped-up" basis that can eliminate the deferred gain. That combination is behind the old saying, "swap till you drop."

## Repairs versus improvements

One more piece of vocabulary will save you real money. A **repair** (patching a roof leak, fixing a faucet, repainting a unit) is generally deductible in the year you pay it. An **improvement** (a new roof, a kitchen renovation, a new parking lot) must be capitalized and depreciated over time. The line between them is a matter of facts and judgment, with rules around whether the work betters the property, adapts it to a new use, or restores it. Small-dollar safe harbors exist for routine maintenance and for items under a threshold per invoice. The practical advice: keep itemized invoices, ask your CPA how to classify large jobs *before* you do them, and don't call a renovation a "repair" on your own. Aggressive classification is a common audit issue and it's easy to avoid with good records.

## The mistakes I see most

**Buying for the tax benefit.** A deduction of $1 saves you perhaps 24 to 37 cents. You spent a dollar to save a quarter. The investment has to stand on its own.

**Assuming losses are usable.** Check passive-loss rules before you assume a loss offsets your salary.

**Forgetting recapture.** Treating depreciation as a permanent gift rather than a deferral leads to nasty surprises at sale.

**Missing the 1031 deadlines.** The 45- and 180-day clocks do not extend. Line up replacement candidates before you list.

**Poor basis allocation.** Allocating too little to the building, or no records of improvements, costs you deductions. Keep closing statements and receipts.

**Using a generalist.** A CPA who doesn't work with rental real estate may miss cost segregation, repair-versus-capital rules, passive-loss planning, and entity issues.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Should you do a cost seg?** If you can use the losses (REPS, or large passive income from other properties), the time value of a big deduction is real money. If you can't, the study can still help by building suspended losses to use at sale, but the benefit is far smaller and you've paid for it now. Run the numbers with your CPA before you order a study.

**Should you do a 1031 or pay the tax and be done?** The 1031 keeps more capital working, but it also forces you to buy something, on a deadline, which pushes people into mediocre deals. A deal chosen to meet a 45-day clock is a weak deal. If nothing good is available, paying 25% to 27% of the gain may be better than a bad purchase.

**Entity and structure.** Most rentals are held in LLCs taxed as pass-throughs. The entity choice matters for liability, financing, and partners, but it does not usually change the tax calculation for a single owner. We'll look at structure in the HoldCo track.

## If you remember nothing else

- Taxable income and cash flow are different numbers. Depreciation creates paper losses, and principal repayments are not deductible.
- Passive-loss rules decide whether you can use a loss now or must carry it forward. Confirm yours before buying for the tax benefit.
- Depreciation is a deferral, not a gift. Recapture and capital gains come at sale; a 1031 can defer them again, within strict deadlines.

## This week

Ask your CPA (or a CPA you're considering hiring) three questions: Would a rental loss be passive for me, and how much could I use? Does cost segregation make sense on a purchase of my size? What's my estimated tax cost if I sold a property I'd held five years? Write down the answers.

# Example 1: Why Maple Court Shows a Loss While It Pays You

## The setup

Maple Court (price $1,600,000, 80% allocated to the building) has a $1,040,000 loan at 6.75%. NOI starts at $104,000 and grows 3% a year. You hold five years.

## The numbers

Depreciation: $1,280,000 / 27.5 = **$46,545** a year (rounded; the first year is slightly less under the mid-month convention).

| Year | NOI | Interest | Depreciation | Taxable income | Cash flow after debt service |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $104,000 | $69,861 | $46,545 | -$12,407 | $23,055 |
| 2 | $107,120 | $69,090 | $46,545 | -$8,515 | $26,175 |
| 3 | $110,334 | $68,264 | $46,545 | -$4,476 | $29,389 |
| 4 | $113,644 | $67,381 | $46,545 | -$283 | $32,699 |
| 5 | $117,053 | $66,437 | $46,545 | $4,071 | $36,108 |

Over five years: cash flow of $147,425; total taxable income of **-$21,610**.

## What the veteran sees

For four years the owner receives $23,055 to $32,699 a year in cash and reports a loss or break-even. Even in year five, the taxable income is only $4,071 on $36,108 of cash. That is why people talk about real estate being tax-efficient: for years, most of the cash flow is effectively sheltered. Notice, too, that the shelter shrinks over time: as the loan amortizes, interest falls by about $3,400 over the five years while NOI rises by $13,000, so the cushion between cash flow and taxable income narrows every year.

How much is the loss worth? If it's usable against other income at a 24% rate, year one's -$12,407 saves $2,978 of tax. At 35%, it saves $4,342. If it's passive and suspended (the surgeon's situation), it saves nothing now. It sits in a carryforward and reduces the taxable gain at sale, a smaller and later benefit.

## Change one thing

Interest rates matter here. If the loan were interest-free (an all-cash purchase), year one's taxable income would be $104,000 - $46,545 = $57,455, positive and fully taxable. Leverage is what turns the building into a "loss." Interest is deductible, principal isn't, and an all-cash buyer has no interest to deduct. Depreciation alone shelters less than half of the NOI.

# Example 2: What Cost Segregation Does

## The setup

Cedar Ridge: price $5,200,000; 80% to building = $4,160,000. A cost segregation study ($14,000) identifies 22% of the building basis ($915,200) as 5-, 7-, and 15-year property. Assume 100% bonus depreciation applies to those components in year one, and the balance depreciates over 27.5 years. For simplicity, use full-year depreciation and an investor who can use the losses at a 35% marginal rate.

## The numbers

| Depreciation | Straight-line only | With cost segregation |
|---|---:|---:|
| Building basis | $4,160,000 | $4,160,000 |
| Reclassified short-life property (22%) | $0 | $915,200 |
| Year-1 deduction on short-life property | $0 | $915,200 |
| Remaining basis $3,244,800 / 27.5 | | $117,993 |
| Full building $4,160,000 / 27.5 | $151,273 | |
| **Year-1 depreciation** | **$151,273** | **$1,033,193** |
| **Extra year-1 deduction** | | **$881,920** |

Taxable income, year 1 (NOI $338,000 less roughly $232,000 of interest):

| | Straight-line | With cost seg |
|---|---:|---:|
| Taxable income | about -$45,000 | about -$927,000 |
| Tax value of the extra deduction at 24% | | $211,661 |
| at 35% | | $308,672 |
| at 37% | | $326,310 |

## What the veteran sees

For an investor who can use the loss, the study produces roughly $300,000 of tax savings in the first year for a $14,000 fee. That's a startling number, and it is why cost seg has become standard on larger deals.

Now the other side of the ledger. First, you borrowed that benefit from the future: the $915,200 you deducted comes back at sale. The short-life components are recaptured as *ordinary income*, up to 37%, not the 25% ceiling on building depreciation. On $915,200 that's about $338,624 versus $228,800: roughly $110,000 more tax than straight-line recapture. Second, it only helps if the loss is usable. If the owner is a doctor with passive-loss limits, nearly all of the $926,000 loss is suspended.

## Change one thing

The investor is not a real estate professional and has no other passive income. Now the extra $881,920 of deductions generates no current tax savings. It becomes a suspended loss that will be used when the property is sold, at which point it offsets the recapture it created. In that case the cost seg changes the timing of nothing, and the $14,000 buys little. Before commissioning a study, get your CPA's answer to: "Can I use the loss this year?"

# Example 3: The Sale, the Recapture, and the 1031

## The setup

You sell Maple Court after five years at $1,854,839 with 3% sale costs ($55,645), and pay off a loan balance of $976,307. Your original tax basis was $1,620,000 (price plus $20,000 of capitalized closing costs). Accumulated depreciation is $232,727 (5 × $46,545). Assume federal rates: 25% on unrecaptured depreciation, 20% on the remaining gain, plus 3.8% net investment income tax on both, and ignore state tax.

## The numbers

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sale price | $1,854,839 |
| Sale costs | -$55,645 |
| Amount realized | $1,799,194 |
| Adjusted basis ($1,620,000 - $232,727) | $1,387,273 |
| **Total taxable gain** | **$411,921** |
| Depreciation recapture portion | $232,727 |
| Remaining capital gain | $179,194 |

| Tax | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| Recapture | $232,727 × (25% + 3.8%) | $67,025 |
| Capital gain | $179,194 × (20% + 3.8%) | $42,648 |
| **Total federal tax** | | **$109,673** (26.6% of gain) |

Net sale proceeds before tax (after loan payoff): $822,886. After tax: $713,213. A state income tax of 5% would add about $20,600.

With a 1031 exchange, you reinvest the $822,886 of equity and buy a replacement property with at least $976,307 of new debt, and the entire $109,673 of tax is deferred. You have **$822,886 working** in the next property instead of $713,213, 15.4% more capital.

## What the veteran sees

The recapture is the piece people forget: 56% of the taxable gain here is just the depreciation you took. You enjoyed the deductions for five years; you pay them back at 28.8%. The rest of the gain, a modest appreciation, is taxed at 23.8%.

The 1031 doesn't forgive the tax; it moves the clock. It makes sense when you have a good replacement property to buy. Its deadlines are unforgiving: if you close the sale on March 1, you must identify replacement properties in writing by April 15 and close on one by August 28. If you close on September 1, the identification deadline is October 16 and the closing deadline is February 28 of the next year, or your return's due date if earlier.

## Change one thing

You can't find a replacement property that meets your standards, and you're offered a mediocre deal at a 5.5% cap rate to complete the exchange. Compare: paying $109,673 of tax and having $713,213 free, versus buying an overpriced asset to avoid it. If the poor deal costs you 1.5 points of return a year on $822,886, that's $12,343 a year; about nine years of that lost return adds up to more than the tax you deferred. A bad exchange can cost more than the tax it defers. The tax is a cost to compare against alternatives, not an enemy to avoid at any price.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

A rental building produces $23,000 of cash after debt service in year one, yet your tax return shows a loss. What best explains this?

- [ ] The IRS allows you to exclude rental income in the first year :: There is no such exclusion. Rental income is generally taxable; the difference comes from deductions.
- [x] Depreciation is deductible without any cash leaving, and principal isn't deductible. :: Depreciation lowers taxable income without reducing cash, while principal repayments reduce cash but aren't expenses. Together they create a gap.
- [ ] Cash flow is always larger than taxable income by law :: There's no such rule; the gap depends on interest, depreciation, and other deductions, and can run either way.
- [ ] Mortgage payments are fully deductible, including principal :: Only the interest portion is deductible. Principal is a repayment of debt.

> Depreciation shelters cash flow. Principal comes out of your pocket but not out of your tax bill.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** You earn $280,000 a year from a full-time job and have no other rental properties. You buy a building that is projected to show a $45,000 taxable loss in year one because of depreciation. You do not qualify as a real estate professional.

What happens to the loss?

- [x] It is usually passive and carried forward until you sell or have passive income. :: With income above the phase-out range for the small-landlord allowance and no REPS, passive-loss rules suspend the loss. It becomes useful later.
- [ ] You deduct the full $45,000 against your salary this year :: That is the treatment for a real estate professional or someone within the allowance. At $280,000 of income, you'd generally not qualify.
- [ ] You deduct up to $25,000 against your salary this year :: The $25,000 allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income, so at $280,000 it is not available.
- [ ] The loss is permanently lost if you don't use it this year :: Suspended passive losses carry forward indefinitely and are released when you sell the property, so they aren't lost.

> Whether a tax loss helps you now depends on who you are, not on what the building does.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

A cost segregation study reclassifies 22% of a building's basis into short-lives and allows a large first-year deduction. What is the main trade-off?

- [ ] It increases the total depreciation over the life of the building :: Total depreciation is capped at your basis. The study changes the timing, moving deductions earlier.
- [ ] It eliminates the need to pay tax on the sale :: It does nothing of the kind. It can make the sale tax larger, because accelerated amounts are recaptured.
- [x] It moves deductions earlier, but the short-life part is recaptured at ordinary rates later. :: The deduction is valuable if you can use it now, but the personal property portion is recaptured as ordinary income, up to 37%.
- [ ] It reduces the property's value for the loan underwriting :: Depreciation is a tax concept and isn't part of the NOI used for loan sizing.

> A tax deduction taken early is a loan from your future self. Know the rate you'll repay it at.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** You close the sale of an investment property on March 1 and want to complete a 1031 exchange. You have a qualified intermediary holding the proceeds, but haven't yet found a replacement property.

Which statement correctly describes your deadlines?

- [ ] You have 180 days to identify and 45 days to close :: The periods are reversed. You have 45 days to identify and 180 days to close.
- [ ] You can identify replacement property whenever you find one, as long as you close within a year :: The identification window is 45 days, and the closing must occur within 180 days or the tax return due date, whichever is earlier.
- [ ] The deadlines run from the date you sign the purchase agreement :: The clocks start at the closing of the property you sold, not from a contract date.
- [x] Identify in writing by April 15; close by about August 28. :: Forty-five days from March 1 is April 15, and 180 days is August 28. Neither deadline extends for weekends or holidays.

> The 1031 clocks start at closing and do not stop. Have candidates in hand before you list.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** You're comparing two deals. Deal A has a 9% projected pre-tax levered IRR and modest depreciation. Deal B has a 6% pre-tax IRR, but a cost segregation study would generate a large first-year loss. Your CPA says you could use the loss against other income at a 35% rate.

How should the tax benefit enter your decision?

- [ ] Choose Deal B, because the tax savings are guaranteed and the IRR is not :: The tax benefit depends on future law, your income, and the hold, and it comes back through recapture. It is not a guaranteed profit.
- [x] Judge both on pre-tax merits, then add the tax effect to each. :: A deduction saves 35 cents per dollar you spent, so it modifies a deal, it doesn't create one. Compare after-tax returns for both, starting from the pre-tax result.
- [ ] Choose Deal A, because tax benefits should be ignored entirely :: Taxes can change a return by several points, so they shouldn't be ignored. They should be added to a sound deal and not replace one.
- [ ] Choose whichever deal has the larger first-year deduction :: The size of the deduction says nothing about whether the investment is sound.

> Buy the investment first. Let the tax code make it a little better.
