# Lesson

Early in my career I was handed an offering memorandum for a 24-unit building with a glossy cover and a one-page "Financial Summary." NOI: $233,000. Asking price: $3.6 million. The cap rate printed in bold was 6.47%. I was twenty-nine and I remember thinking how clean it looked. A friend of mine, twenty years older, glanced at the page for about ninety seconds and said, "Where's the management fee?" There wasn't one. "Where's payroll?" There wasn't one. "What does the insurance cost today, not what did it cost in 2019?" Nobody had asked.

We rebuilt the statement over a weekend. The NOI came out at $178,000. At the same 6.5% cap rate, the building was worth about $2.74 million, not $3.6 million. The difference between the document and the truth was $856,000, and none of it was fraud. Every line in the seller's statement was a number somebody had paid or collected. It was simply a statement built to sell a building, and my friend's two questions were the reason I kept my money.

That is the whole skill of this module. A property's value is its NOI divided by a cap rate, so every dollar of NOI you get wrong is worth fifteen dollars of price. Learn to read these statements like a forensic accountant.

## What NOI is, line by line

Net operating income is what the property earns from operating, before debt and before income taxes. Start at the top:

- **Gross potential rent (GPR):** every unit rented at full market or in-place rent, 100% occupied, all year.
- **Vacancy and credit loss:** the rent you won't collect because units are empty or tenants don't pay. Use the larger of the building's actual history and the market's stabilized figure, usually 5% to 8% for apartments, never zero.
- **Other income:** laundry, parking, pet fees, late fees, utility reimbursements. Count only what appears in the bank deposits.
- **Effective gross income (EGI):** GPR minus vacancy and credit loss, plus other income.
- **Operating expenses:** the costs to run the place, from taxes to trash.
- **Net operating income:** EGI minus operating expenses.

NOI deliberately excludes mortgage payments, depreciation, income taxes, and big capital projects. It's the property's earning power with the financing and ownership structure stripped out, so two buyers with different loans can compare the same building on the same footing.

## The expenses sellers miss

Across all the statements I've picked apart, the same lines are wrong over and over:

**Management fee.** If the owner manages the building themselves, there's no line, and the seller's NOI looks several points better. A buyer who hires management will pay 4% to 8% of collections for apartments, and 3% to 5% for larger commercial properties. If you manage it yourself, you're paying yourself with your time, and the market will still price the building as though a manager is in place. Always include a management fee at the market rate.

**Payroll.** On-site staff, a part-time maintenance person, a leasing agent. The seller may have been doing it unpaid. A lender will add the cost back.

**Property taxes.** In many jurisdictions a sale triggers reassessment, so the seller's tax bill reflects an old, lower assessed value. Your bill will be based on your purchase price. Find out how it works locally and calculate the new bill; taxes are usually the biggest line, and a 15% to 30% jump is not unusual.

**Insurance.** Quotes can differ wildly from the seller's premium, especially after a few claim-heavy years in the market. Get a quote before you make an offer, not after.

**Repairs and maintenance and turnover.** Old owners defer. A statement showing $600 a unit in repairs on a 40-year-old building is telling you something: either the building is a miracle or the work isn't being done. Normal ranges for older apartments run $1,000 to $1,800 per unit including turnover costs.

**Replacement reserves.** Roofs, boilers, parking lots, water heaters. Careful buyers and lenders deduct $250 to $350 per unit per year as a reserve before calculating the NOI they use for value. It's not an accounting expense, but you will spend it. Pretending otherwise is borrowing from the future.

A quick sanity check that costs nothing: the **expense ratio**, operating expenses divided by EGI. Older apartment buildings with owner-paid utilities usually land between 45% and 55%. Newer ones with tenant-paid utilities may be 35% to 45%. If the statement in front of you shows 37% on a 1980s building, the building is not unusually efficient. Something is missing.

## T-12, T-3, and the pro forma

Sellers hand you three kinds of financials, and they deserve three levels of trust.

- **The T-12** (trailing twelve months) is a month-by-month record of what actually came in and went out. It's the best starting point, but it's only as good as the books behind it. Always verify it against bank statements.
- **The T-3** (trailing three months, annualized) is the freshest view of the property's run rate. If the last three months are weaker than the previous nine, the T-12 is flattering you.
- **The pro forma** is the seller's or broker's projection of where things will go. It's a marketing document. It might be right; assume it isn't until you've rebuilt it yourself.

My rule: underwrite from the T-12 and the T-3, add the expenses that you'll face but the seller didn't, and rebuild the revenue from the rent roll. Use the pro forma only for what you intend to *change*, and justify those changes with evidence.

## Cap rates: the price of income

The cap rate is simply NOI divided by price. Flip it and you have the working formula: **value = NOI / cap rate**. A $178,000 NOI is worth $2.74 million at a 6.5% cap rate, $2.97 million at 6.0%, and $2.55 million at 7.0%. Each half-point of cap rate moves the price by 7% to 8%.

But what *is* a cap rate? It's the market's required yield for a given type of income, in a given place, with a given level of risk. Roughly, it's the risk-free rate plus a premium for illiquidity, operating risk, and the chance that income won't grow. That's why a 10-year Treasury at 4.25% and a cap rate of 6.5% (a 225 basis-point spread) both move together over time, but not one-for-one. When rates rise 100 basis points, cap rates typically rise 30 to 70 basis points as the market digests it; buyers' required returns rise, but rents often grow and soften the blow. Don't assume cap rates move in lockstep with Treasuries. Don't assume they don't.

What you must never do is take a cap rate from a listing and apply it to the *seller's* NOI. The cap rate is only meaningful when it's applied to a NOI built under the same assumptions as the comparable sales: management fee in, reserves in, taxes reassessed. If the comps were underwritten with a 5% management fee and your NOI has none, you're comparing apples to oranges.

## Rebuild the revenue from the rent roll, not the summary

The expense side gets the attention, but revenue errors are just as expensive. The rent roll lists every unit, its tenant, its rent, its lease dates, and its status. Rebuild income from it and then compare to what hit the bank:

- **Add up the in-place rents** and compare to the seller's gross potential rent. If the summary says $374,400 and the rent roll says $361,000, someone is counting vacant units at market rent or including a unit that is actually down for repairs.
- **Look at concessions and loss-to-lease.** A unit renting at $1,250 against a market of $1,300 is loss-to-lease of $50; a unit with a month free is $1,300 on paper and $1,192 in cash.
- **Check collections.** Bad debt of 1% sounds small; at 4% on a $360,000 income line, it's $14,400, and in tougher neighborhoods I've seen 6% to 8%.
- **Match deposits to the rent roll.** Add up twelve months of bank deposits. If they come in 5% below the rent roll after vacancy, the difference has an explanation, and you should hear it before you close.

The test I use on every deal is simple: *total deposits for twelve months should reconcile to the T-12 income within about 2%.* If they don't, you don't have a financial statement; you have a theory.

## Not all cap rates are the same cap rate

When someone says "cap rate," ask which one. The **going-in cap rate** is first-year NOI divided by price, and it depends on which NOI is used. The **stabilized cap rate** uses NOI after you've fixed vacancy and raised rents, so it's higher than going-in on a value-add deal. The **exit cap rate** is the one you assume a future buyer will pay, and it's the most dangerous assumption in the model. And **yield on cost** (stabilized NOI divided by your total cost including renovation) is what a developer or value-add investor compares to the market cap rate to see if the project creates value. A project that costs $4 million all-in and produces $300,000 of stabilized NOI has a 7.5% yield on cost. If similar stabilized buildings trade at a 6.0% cap rate, you've created about $1 million of value (that is, $5,000,000 minus $4,000,000), and that gap is your margin of safety and your profit.

## The mistakes I see most

**Trusting the seller's expense lines without bank statements.** A statement is a story. Bank statements and invoices are the evidence. Even honest sellers misclassify or smooth. If they won't provide bank statements, you've learned something.

**Capitalizing a number that isn't recurring.** One-time income, such as a settlement, a big laundry-contract signing bonus, or a month of unusual late fees, doesn't belong in NOI. Neither do one-time expenses *if* they're genuinely one-time. Review each line and ask whether it will happen again.

**Using the going-in cap rate for the exit.** If you buy at a 6.5% cap rate, you should not assume you'll sell at 6.5%. The building will be older and the market may be different. Most careful underwriters assume an exit cap rate 25 to 75 basis points higher than the entry.

**Ignoring the trend inside the T-12.** Averages hide direction. A year that started strong and ended weak averages out to something that looks fine.

**Forgetting that your price changes your taxes.** The tax bill the seller has been paying is not the tax bill you'll pay if the jurisdiction reassesses on sale. Your offer price feeds back into your expenses.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**When to ignore the seller's number entirely.** If you find three material errors in the seller's financials, stop adjusting and start rebuilding from scratch, or walk. People who are careless in three places are careless in a fourth place you haven't found.

**What a broker's cap rate really means.** When a broker quotes you a cap rate, ask: on what NOI? Trailing 12, in-place, or pro forma? With what management fee, and what reserves? The same building can be quoted at a 5.2% cap or a 6.8% cap depending on which answers they use. Always ask for the NOI and rebuild the cap rate yourself.

**Replacement cost as a sanity check.** If a building trades at $150,000 a door and it costs $225,000 a door to build the same thing today, your downside is cushioned, because new competition can't be priced below you. If you pay $240,000 a door for an older building when new construction costs $225,000, you're paying a premium to own something worse. Cap rates are the language of income; replacement cost is the language of safety.

## If you remember nothing else

- Value = NOI / cap rate. Every dollar of NOI you misjudge is worth roughly fifteen dollars of price.
- Rebuild the statement yourself: add the management fee, payroll, reassessed taxes, a real insurance quote, and reserves, then check the expense ratio.
- Underwrite from the T-12 and T-3, verified against bank statements. The pro forma is a sales document.

## This week

Pull any apartment listing with a stated NOI. Re-create the operating statement from the seller's numbers, then add what's missing: a 5% management fee, $250 per unit in reserves, and a 20% increase in property taxes. Recalculate NOI, and at the cap rate in the listing, compute the price your NOI would justify.

# Example 1: The $856,000 Gap

## The setup

You're evaluating Birchwood, a 24-unit apartment building asking $3,600,000. Average rent is $1,300 a month. The seller's package shows NOI of $233,068, a 6.47% cap rate. The building is 1980s construction, and the seller manages it personally. You rebuild the statement.

## The numbers

| Line | Seller | Your underwriting | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Gross potential rent (24 × $1,300 × 12) | $374,400 | $374,400 | $0 |
| Vacancy and credit loss | -$11,232 (3%) | -$22,464 (6%) | -$11,232 |
| Other income | $10,800 | $9,600 | -$1,200 |
| **Effective gross income** | **$373,968** | **$361,536** | **-$12,432** |
| Property taxes | $42,000 | $48,000 | -$6,000 |
| Insurance | $17,500 | $21,000 | -$3,500 |
| Utilities | $31,500 | $33,000 | -$1,500 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $33,600 | $33,600 | $0 |
| Admin and marketing | $6,500 | $6,500 | $0 |
| Landscaping and snow | $9,800 | $9,800 | $0 |
| On-site payroll | $0 | $7,200 | -$7,200 |
| Management (5% of EGI) | $0 | $18,077 | -$18,077 |
| Reserves ($250 per unit) | $0 | $6,000 | -$6,000 |
| **Total expenses** | **$140,900** | **$183,177** | **-$42,277** |
| **NOI** | **$233,068** | **$178,359** | **-$54,709** |

The seller's expense ratio is 37.7%. Yours is 50.7%.

| Cap rate | Value at your NOI ($178,359) | Gap to $3,600,000 ask |
|---|---:|---:|
| 6.0% | $2,972,653 | -$627,347 |
| 6.5% | $2,743,988 | -$856,012 |
| 7.0% | $2,547,989 | -$1,052,011 |

At the asking price, your NOI implies a cap rate of 4.95%.

## What the veteran sees

A 37.7% expense ratio on a 1980s building with owner-paid utilities is the first red flag, before you've opened a single bank statement. Then the gaps: no management, no payroll, no reserves (together $31,277), taxes that won't survive a sale, and an insurance premium that predates today's market. Vacancy of 3% is possible in a hot market, but not as a stabilized assumption.

Nothing here is dishonest. Every number is a figure that someone really paid. It's just a statement for a seller who doesn't pay himself and doesn't replace anything. It would be wrong to hold it against him, and a mistake to buy on it.

The price you'd offer depends on the cap rate you believe the market pays for this property. At 6.5%, the building is worth around $2.74 million. Your opening offer lives in the neighborhood of $2.7 million, and the conversation is about whether the seller understands why.

## Change one thing

Suppose the jurisdiction does *not* reassess taxes on sale, and the seller's $42,000 stands. Your NOI rises $6,000 to $184,359, and the 6.5% value to $2.84 million. A single local rule moved the price by $92,000. Check how taxes work before you bid, not after.

# Example 2: The T-12 That Flatters

## The setup

Linden is a 20-unit building. The seller's T-12 shows effective gross income of $294,000 and expenses of $138,000, an NOI of $156,000. At a 6.75% cap rate, you'd value it at $2,311,111. But you ask for the month-by-month detail and the bank statements.

## The numbers

Collections by month: the first nine months averaged $24,900. The last three averaged $23,300. Insurance renewed in month 10 at a rate $4,200 a year higher than the prior premium.

| Measure | EGI | Expenses | NOI | Value at 6.75% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| T-12 as reported | $294,000 | $138,000 | $156,000 | $2,311,111 |
| T-3 annualized run rate ($23,300 × 12) | $279,600 | $142,200 | $137,400 | $2,035,556 |
| Difference | -$14,400 | +$4,200 | -$18,600 (-11.9%) | -$275,556 |

## What the veteran sees

The T-12 averages two different buildings: one that was collecting $24,900 a month and one that's collecting $23,300. Something changed in the last quarter: maybe a few units went vacant, maybe a couple of tenants stopped paying, maybe the seller stopped chasing late rent because he's about to sell. You don't know yet, and the right response is to find out, not to average it away.

The insurance renewal is a fixed cost that wasn't in the full 12 months. The T-12 only shows a partial year of the higher premium. Your first-year cost will be the full $4,200 more.

A buyer who capitalizes the T-12 overpays by about $275,000. A buyer who capitalizes the run rate pays what the building earns now.

## Change one thing

You dig in and discover the last three months are weak because of one-time vacancies from a flood in two units, now repaired and re-leased at the same rents. The run rate isn't permanent. If you can document that, you can pay closer to the T-12, but you'd still deduct the insurance change. The answer depends on the explanation, so ask for it in writing and test it against the rent roll and bank deposits.

# Example 3: What a Cap Rate Move Does to Price, and What Replacement Cost Says

## The setup

You're evaluating a different building with a stabilized NOI of $200,000. You want to see how sensitive its price is to cap rates and what happens under different assumptions about how interest rates feed through to caps. The 10-year Treasury yield is 4.25%, and cap rates for similar buildings are 6.5% (a 225 basis-point spread). New construction costs about $225,000 per door to build, and this building has 24 units.

## The numbers

| Cap rate | Value of $200,000 NOI | Value per door (24 units) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 5.5% | $3,636,364 | $151,515 |
| 6.0% | $3,333,333 | $138,889 |
| 6.5% | $3,076,923 | $128,205 |
| 7.0% | $2,857,143 | $119,048 |
| 7.5% | $2,666,667 | $111,111 |
| 8.0% | $2,500,000 | $104,167 |

Now suppose the 10-year rises 100 basis points to 5.25%.

| Pass-through of the rate move to cap rates | New cap rate | Value | Change from $3,076,923 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| None | 6.5% | $3,076,923 | 0% |
| Half (50 bps) | 7.0% | $2,857,143 | -7.1% |
| Full (100 bps) | 7.5% | $2,666,667 | -13.3% |

## What the veteran sees

The value-per-door column is the one I'd stare at. At a 6.5% cap, the building trades at $128,205 a door, against a replacement cost of $225,000. You're buying at 57% of what it would cost to build it new. That cushions you: no developer can build competing space and rent it cheaply enough to hurt you without losing money. It doesn't guarantee anything, but it makes the downside more bounded than a building priced near replacement cost.

The pass-through table is the sensitivity that matters. The same $200,000 NOI is worth between $2.67 million and $3.08 million depending on a market view you can't observe. That's why disciplined buyers underwrite the exit at a higher cap rate than the entry and why they pay attention to the debt they take on. A half-point of cap rate is worth about $220,000 here.

## Change one thing

Imagine your income grows 3% a year for five years and the cap rate rises from 6.5% to 7.0%. Year-six NOI is $231,855 and the exit value is $3,312,212, still 7.6% higher than today's $3,076,923, even with cap rate expansion. Growth in income can outrun a modest cap rate rise. It can't outrun a large one, and it can't outrun it if the income growth never comes.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

An offering memorandum shows a 1980s apartment building with owner-paid utilities and an expense ratio of 37%. What is the most useful first reaction?

- [ ] The building is unusually efficient, which is a selling point to underwrite. :: That's the seller's likely framing, but a 1980s building with owner-paid utilities usually runs 45% to 55%. An outlier ratio usually means missing costs, not magic.
- [x] Ask which expenses are missing, then check the statement against bank records. :: A ratio that far below the norm usually means management, payroll, reserves, or deferred repairs are absent. Bank statements will show what was actually paid.
- [ ] Apply a higher cap rate to the NOI to compensate for the uncertainty. :: A higher cap rate on a wrong NOI hides the problem rather than fixing it. The first job is to find the correct NOI, then choose a cap rate.
- [ ] Wait for the pro forma, since it will show corrected figures. :: The pro forma is a projection built by the same party. It tends to correct nothing and add optimistic changes.

> An expense ratio far from the norm is a question, not a feature. Find out what's missing.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** You've rebuilt a building's NOI to $178,359 after adding a 5% management fee, payroll, reserves, and reassessed taxes. Comparable sales suggest the market is paying a 6.5% cap rate for similar buildings, underwritten the same way. The seller is asking $3,600,000.

What is the NOI-supported value, and what does it say about the asking price?

- [ ] About $3.6 million, because the seller's price reflects local demand. :: A price reflects an asking position, not an NOI. Without income to support it, the price is a hope.
- [x] About $2.74 million, so the asking price is roughly $856,000 too high. :: $178,359 / 0.065 = $2,743,988. The $856,012 gap is the price of income that isn't there once the statement is rebuilt.
- [ ] About $2.74 million, which means the seller will accept your full-price offer. :: The arithmetic is right but the conclusion isn't. The gap is a negotiating position, and the seller may not accept it.
- [ ] About $2.97 million, because you should use a 6.0% cap rate for a conservative view. :: A lower cap rate gives a higher value, which is the aggressive direction, not the conservative one. A more conservative view would use a higher rate.

> Value = NOI / cap rate, but only if NOI was built the way the comps were built.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

Why do careful buyers and lenders deduct replacement reserves, often $250 to $350 per apartment per year, before calculating the NOI they capitalize?

- [ ] Because reserves are an accounting expense under standard rules :: Reserves are typically a below-the-line capital item, not an accounting operating expense. They're included in underwriting for a different reason.
- [ ] Because lenders require the reserve account to be funded at closing :: Some do, but that's a separate mechanism. The underwriting deduction exists regardless of whether a reserve account is funded.
- [ ] Because it makes the cap rate come out lower :: Deducting reserves lowers NOI, which lowers the value at a given cap rate. It doesn't change the cap rate itself.
- [x] Because roofs, boilers, and paving will need replacing, and the buyer pays for that. :: These costs are certain to occur but irregular. Smoothing them into an annual number prevents the income stream from looking richer than it is.

> A cost you will certainly pay should be in the number you pay for.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** The seller's T-12 for a 20-unit building shows NOI of $156,000. The monthly detail shows collections averaged $24,900 for the first nine months and $23,300 for the last three. Insurance renewed in month 10, adding $4,200 a year versus the prior premium. The seller says the recent drop is "just seasonal." You're valuing at a 6.75% cap rate.

Which NOI should you underwrite until you learn more?

- [ ] $156,000, because the T-12 is the standard measure of NOI :: The T-12 is a starting point. Averaging a stronger period with a weaker one overstates the current run rate.
- [ ] $146,700, the midpoint between the T-12 and the run rate :: Splitting the difference is arbitrary. It has no connection to why the income fell or what the future will look like.
- [x] About $137,400, the annualized last three months with the full insurance cost. :: $23,300 × 12 is $279,600, less $142,200 of expenses, equals $137,400. Until the seller documents a temporary cause, the freshest data is the best evidence.
- [ ] $180,000, because the drop is probably seasonal and will recover :: This assumes the explanation without evidence. Residential rent collections rarely fall for seasonal reasons and rise back, and it would put you above even the T-12.

> Averages smooth, run rates reveal. Underwrite the run rate until someone shows you why it is wrong.

## Q5 | multiple_choice

You buy a building at a 6.5% cap rate and plan to sell in five years. Which exit cap rate assumption is most defensible?

- [ ] 6.0%, since your improvements will make the building more desirable :: Assuming cap rate compression turns a hope into a core assumption. If your improvements raise NOI, that is already captured in the NOI.
- [ ] 6.5%, the same as the entry, since the market is stable :: It sounds neutral, but it ignores that the building will be five years older. Many underwriters treat this as the aggressive end of reasonable.
- [x] 7.0% to 7.25%, reflecting an older building and uncertain markets :: Adding 50 to 75 basis points to the entry cap builds in aging and market uncertainty, and a deal that works at that exit has a margin of safety.
- [ ] Whatever the broker suggests, since brokers know the market :: Brokers' interests are in selling the property and keeping you interested. Your exit assumption is the most sensitive in your model, and it should be yours.

> Underwrite the exit as a skeptic. If a deal needs cap rate compression to work, it is a bet on the market, not an investment in the building.
