# Lesson

A few years before the last big downturn, a dentist I'll call Ray bought a twelve-unit building. He was smart, he saved hard, and he had been told his whole life that real estate only goes up. He paid $780,000, put down 20%, and financed the rest on an adjustable loan. The broker's flyer said the building "cash flows from day one." It did, by about $140 a month, if you ignored the roof he'd have to replace in three years, the vacancy the flyer assumed was 3%, and the fact that the loan reset 1.75 points higher in year two.

Ray's building did not collapse. It just never made him a dime. Five years in, he'd put in another $41,000 of his own money to keep the lights on, the value was flat, and he sold for roughly what he paid, minus the commissions. His "investment" had cost him five years and about forty grand. When I asked him what went wrong, he said, "I thought it would appreciate." That sentence has cost people more money than every bad roof and bad tenant combined.

So let's start where Ray should have started: what, exactly, are you buying, and where does the money come from?

## You are buying an income stream

A building is wood, concrete, and dirt. You don't care about any of that, or you shouldn't. What you are buying is a stream of rent checks, net of what it costs to keep the building running, and a claim on whoever buys that stream from you later. Everything in this course follows from that one idea.

That stream has a name: **net operating income**, or NOI. It's the rent and other income the property collects, minus the operating expenses to run it (taxes, insurance, repairs, management, utilities), before any mortgage payment. We'll rebuild NOI properly in Module 4. For now, hold on to the idea that the price you pay is a multiple of that stream. If someone will pay $1,600,000 for $104,000 of NOI, the buyer is accepting a **cap rate** of 6.5% ($104,000 / $1,600,000). The cap rate is the yield on the whole building if you paid cash.

Here's the building we'll use all through this module. I'll call it Maple Court: sixteen apartments, average rent $1,075 a month.

| Maple Court, year one | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Gross potential rent (16 × $1,075 × 12) | $206,400 |
| Vacancy and credit loss (6%) | -$12,384 |
| Other income (laundry, fees) | $4,800 |
| **Effective gross income** | **$198,816** |
| Operating expenses (taxes, insurance, repairs, payroll, management, utilities, reserves) | -$94,816 |
| **Net operating income** | **$104,000** |

Price: $1,600,000, or $100,000 a door. Cap rate: 6.5%. That expense line is 47.7% of income, which is about where a building like this lands. Keep an eye on it; more deals die in that line than anywhere else.

## The four engines

When someone says real estate "makes money," they're pointing at four different machines. They behave differently, and the single most useful thing I can teach you is to know which of them is carrying a given deal.

**Engine 1: Cash flow.** NOI minus your debt payments. This is the money that lands in your account while you own the thing. At Maple Court, with a $1,040,000 loan (65% of price) at 6.75% over a 30-year amortization, the payment is $6,745 a month, or $80,945 a year. Cash flow in year one: $104,000 - $80,945 = **$23,055**.

**Engine 2: Principal paydown.** Every payment includes a slice of principal. In year one, of that $80,945, about $69,861 is interest and **$11,084** pays the loan down. That's real wealth creation, but you only get it back when you sell or refinance. It's forced savings, funded by your tenants.

**Engine 3: Appreciation.** The building is worth more later than you paid. This has two very different parents. *Market appreciation* is the tide: rents rise because the area gets richer, or cap rates compress because money got cheaper. You don't control it. *Forced appreciation* is what you do: raise NOI by fixing operations, renovating units, or adding income, and then the building is worth more because NOI is higher. At a 6.5% cap, every extra $1,000 of NOI is worth $15,385 of value. That math is why operators love value-add.

**Engine 4: Tax benefits.** Depreciation lets you deduct a slice of the building's cost every year even though the building isn't wearing out. If $1,280,000 of the $1,600,000 is allocated to the building (the rest is land), the annual deduction is $1,280,000 / 27.5 = **$46,545**. Maple Court's taxable income might be close to zero or negative even while it throws off $23,055 of cash. We'll go deep on this in Module 11, but know now that it's a real engine, and that the IRS gets some of it back when you sell.

Add them up for year one and you don't get a spectacular number. Cash-on-cash return is $23,055 on $592,000 of equity (the $560,000 down payment plus $32,000 of closing costs), or **3.9%**. Then principal paydown adds roughly 1.9 points. Then it depends on engines 3 and 4. A year-one return that looks mediocre on its own is normal. The money is in how the engines compound over five to ten years, and, above all, in what you paid at the start.

## Leverage: the amplifier that doesn't care which way you're facing

Leverage is borrowing to own more than you could buy with your own cash. It magnifies whatever the underlying asset does. If the building returns more than the loan costs, leverage lifts your return. If it returns less, leverage drags it down.

Here's the test most people never run. Maple Court yields 6.5% unlevered. The loan costs 6.75% in interest, and with amortization the annual payment is $80,945 on a $1,040,000 loan, a **debt constant** of 7.78%. You are borrowing at a 7.78% annual cost to own something that yields 6.5%. That's **negative leverage**: in year one, the borrowed money is dragging your yield below what the building would earn with no loan at all.

Is that a bad deal? Not automatically. You may be buying for growth: if NOI rises 3% a year, the yield on cost climbs and the loan stays fixed, so the spread flips in your favor by year three or four. But you must know you're making that bet. In Example 2 you'll see the same building at 0%, 3%, and 5% growth, levered and unlevered, and you'll watch leverage turn from a drag into a rocket depending on one assumption.

Cheap debt in the 2010s made almost every deal look brilliant, because the debt constant was far below cap rates. When rates rose, buyers who had underwritten to that spread discovered they'd been paid for being in a bull market, not for being good at real estate.

## You make your money when you buy

Old-timers say it: you make your money when you buy. It sounds like a platitude until you do the math. Buy Maple Court at $1,600,000 and you need things to go right to earn a double-digit return. Buy the identical building for $1,450,000 (a 7.2% cap) because the seller had a health problem and wanted out, and you own the same income at a lower basis, with a cushion in every scenario. The tenants don't know what you paid. The building doesn't care. But your return is permanently different.

That doesn't mean you should wait around for distress. It means your edge is built in three places: the price you pay relative to the income, the terms of the debt, and the way you operate the asset after closing. Appreciation and market timing are not on that list, because you don't control them. A disciplined buyer underwrites as if they'll never get any market appreciation and treats any that shows up as a gift.

## The mistakes I see most

**Underwriting appreciation as a plan.** Ray's mistake. If your deal only works when values rise, you aren't investing, you're forecasting. The test: set growth at 0% and exit cap rate 50 to 100 basis points higher than today's. If you'd survive that, fine. If you wouldn't, the deal is a bet.

**Confusing NOI with cash in your pocket.** NOI doesn't include your mortgage payment, your capital expenditures (new roof, new boiler, parking lot), or your leasing costs. A building with $104,000 of NOI and $60,000 of annual debt service and a roof due in 2 years isn't throwing off $44,000 in free cash.

**Spending the closing costs in your head and not in your spreadsheet.** On a deal like Maple Court, loan fees, legal, title, inspections, and lender reserves easily run 2% of price. That's $32,000 that doesn't buy you a thing but is gone.

**Buying with no reserves.** The fastest way to lose a good building is to get squeezed for cash in month fourteen. A transmission-sized surprise, a vacancy bump, and a bank that doesn't give you the benefit of the doubt. Fund reserves before you close.

**Mistaking equity for return.** "I've got $400,000 of equity in that building" is a statement about net worth, not about return. If that $400,000 is earning 2% because the building barely clears its debt, you'd be better off with it in Treasury bills.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Cash flow versus growth.** There's no right answer, only a right answer for you. If you're fifty-eight and want income, a 7% cash-on-cash in a slow-growth market is a fine life. If you're thirty-two with a salary and a long runway, you can accept 2% cash flow in a market where rents compound at 5%, provided you can survive the lean years. Know which game you're playing before you screen deals; I've watched people buy the wrong game for their stage of life and spend a decade unhappy.

**How much of your return is in the sale?** Take the five-year total return and ask what fraction arrives only when you sell. In Maple Court's base case, it's about 85%. That's not a flaw, it's the nature of the asset. But when that figure creeps to 95% or more, you're holding a single future event in your hand. The less you can control that event, the more reserves and patience you need.

**The 1% rule and its limits.** Investors use a crude screen: monthly rent should be at least 1% of purchase price. Maple Court passes at about 1.08% ($1,075 against $100,000 a door). It's a fine first filter in the middle of the country. But it ignores expenses entirely. A building in a high-tax, high-insurance metro can clear 1% and still deliver a 3.5% cap rate, while one at 0.8% in a low-tax market can run a 7% cap. Use the 1% rule to decide what to look at, never to decide what to buy.

## If you remember nothing else

- The four engines are cash flow, principal paydown, appreciation, and taxes. Know which two are carrying the deal and how much of each is in your control.
- Leverage multiplies whatever is true. Check your debt constant against the cap rate before you assume it helps you.
- Underwrite as though the market gives you nothing. Then you are in the business of buying well and operating well, which is the part you control.

# Example 1: The Four Engines on Maple Court

## The setup

You buy Maple Court for $1,600,000 with a $1,040,000 loan at 6.75%, 30-year amortization, and $32,000 of closing costs, so you invest $592,000 of equity. You expect NOI to grow 3% a year (small rent increases, flat-ish expenses) and you plan to sell after five years at the same 6.5% cap rate you bought at, paying 3% in sale costs. You'll see below how much each engine contributes.

## The numbers

| Year | NOI (3% growth) | Debt service | Cash flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $104,000 | $80,945 | $23,055 |
| 2 | $107,120 | $80,945 | $26,175 |
| 3 | $110,334 | $80,945 | $29,389 |
| 4 | $113,644 | $80,945 | $32,699 |
| 5 | $117,053 | $80,945 | $36,108 |
| **Total** | | | **$147,425** |

Sale at the end of year five uses year-six NOI: $120,565 / 0.065 = **$1,854,839**.

| Sale math | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sale price | $1,854,839 |
| Sale costs (3%) | -$55,645 |
| Loan balance after 60 payments | -$976,307 |
| **Net sale proceeds** | **$822,886** |

Of the $976,307 remaining balance, you paid down $63,693 of principal over five years.

| Where the five-year profit came from | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash flow (engine 1) | $147,425 |
| Principal paydown (engine 2) | $63,693 |
| Appreciation (engine 3) | $254,839 |
| Sale costs (3%) | -$55,645 |
| Closing costs at purchase | -$32,000 |
| **Total profit** | **$378,311** |

Check it the other way: you received $147,425 of cash flow plus $822,886 of sale proceeds, $970,311 in all, against $592,000 invested. Profit is $378,311, a 1.64x equity multiple and an 11.1% IRR. Both views agree, which is what you want. (Taxes are a fourth engine; they change what you keep, not what the property earns, so they're not in this table.)

## What the veteran sees

Look at how the profit splits: cash flow supplies 39% of it, principal paydown 17%, and appreciation (net of sale and closing costs) the remaining 44%. If you strip out the 3% growth, nothing is left but a loan paydown and a thin cash flow. This is a growth deal wearing a cash-flow costume, and the growth comes mostly from your operating the building (raising rents on rollover, trimming expenses), which you partly control. That's why 11% IRR here is more trustworthy than 11% from market appreciation.

## Change one thing

Say you can only grow NOI 1% a year. Year-six NOI becomes $109,305 and the sale price drops to $1,681,616. After sale costs of $50,448 and the loan payoff, net proceeds are roughly $654,860. Add cash flow of about $125,780 and the total received is $780,640 on $592,000 invested: a 1.32x multiple and roughly a 6.1% IRR. Two points of annual growth is worth five points of IRR. That's the sensitivity to remember.

# Example 2: Leverage Cuts Both Ways

## The setup

Same building, same price. You compare two buyers. Buyer A pays all cash: $1,600,000 plus $20,000 of closing costs. Buyer B uses the $1,040,000 loan and invests $592,000. Both sell after five years at a 6.5% cap rate with 3% sale costs. The only thing you vary is how fast NOI grows.

## The numbers

| NOI growth | Sale price (year 5) | All-cash IRR | Levered IRR | Levered equity multiple |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0% | $1,600,000 | 5.7% | 3.4% | 1.17x |
| 3% | $1,854,839 | 8.6% | 11.1% | 1.64x |
| 5% | $2,042,051 | 10.6% | 15.7% | 1.98x |

Year-one yield on cost: unlevered 6.5%. Levered cash-on-cash: 3.9%.

## What the veteran sees

At zero growth, the all-cash buyer earns 5.7% and the levered buyer 3.4%. Leverage hurt, because the debt constant (7.78%) exceeds the cap rate (6.5%) and there's no growth to bridge the gap. At 3% growth, leverage adds 2.5 points. At 5% growth, it adds 5.1 points.

That is the whole story of leverage: it's a bet that the asset's growth will beat the cost of the money. The leveraged buyer isn't smarter in the 5% scenario; they're just more exposed to the thing that went right. When it goes wrong, the same exposure bites. This is also why lenders care so much about how far NOI can fall before the loan can't be paid, which we'll quantify in Module 5.

## Change one thing

Cut the interest rate to 5.25% (a rate environment like the late 2010s) and the annual debt service on $1,040,000 falls to about $68,915, a debt constant of 6.63%. Now even at 0% growth, the levered buyer's year-one cash flow is $35,085 and the loan helps from day one. The same building looks like a different deal because debt got $12,000 a year cheaper. That's why timing of rates dominated returns for a decade, and why you should never confuse a good rate environment with a good deal.

# Example 3: When the Cap Rate Moves and the Rent Doesn't

## The setup

Two years after buying, you want to sell Maple Court. Rents are as expected: NOI is $107,120 (year two, 3% growth). But interest rates have moved and buyers now require a higher yield: cap rates have expanded from 6.5% to 7.5%. You haven't done anything wrong operationally.

## The numbers

| Cap rate | Value at today's NOI ($107,120) | Change vs. 6.5% |
|---|---:|---:|
| 6.5% | $1,648,000 | n/a |
| 7.0% | $1,530,286 | -$117,714 |
| 7.5% | $1,428,267 | -$219,733 |

Your loan balance after two years is about $1,017,061 (you've paid down roughly $22,900). At a 7.5% cap, a sale at $1,428,267 less 3% costs ($42,848) and loan payoff leaves you about **$368,000** from the $592,000 you put in, before counting the roughly $49,200 of cash flow you've collected. Your equity fell by more than a third, even though rents grew and you did everything right.

## What the veteran sees

A one-point cap rate move, from 6.5% to 7.5%, takes about 13% off the building's value. Because the loan stays the same size, that 13% of value comes entirely out of your equity, and your equity is only about a third of the purchase price. So a 13% hit to value becomes a roughly 37% hit to your capital ($219,733 against $592,000). That's leverage in the other direction.

The practical consequences are bigger than the paper loss. A lender looking at the property today would size a new loan on the lower value, so refinancing could require you to bring cash to the table. If your loan matures into that market, you may face a forced sale. This is exactly the failure that sinks levered investors in rate-shock periods: the building is fine, the income is fine, but the loan comes due at the wrong time.

## Change one thing

You wait. You hold for five years instead of two, grow NOI to $120,565 (3% a year), and the market eases back to a 7.0% cap. The value is $1,722,350, still $132,000 *below* the 6.5%-cap scenario in Example 1, but your five-year IRR is still about 7.9% (a 1.42x multiple) because cash flow and growth did their work. Time is the lender's enemy only if you have to sell on the lender's schedule. Long loan terms and reserves are what buy you the option to wait.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

Maple Court has a 6.5% cap rate. The $1,040,000 loan at 6.75% with a 30-year amortization carries an annual payment of $80,945, a debt constant of about 7.78%. What does that tell you in year one?

- [ ] The deal is overpriced and should be avoided, because borrowing costs more than the property yields. :: Negative leverage in year one isn't automatically a reason to pass. If NOI grows, the spread can turn positive; the point is to know you're underwriting growth.
- [x] Leverage is dragging year one below 6.5%, so growth must carry it. :: The debt costs 7.78% a year in cash while the property yields 6.5%, so each borrowed dollar drags the yield. The deal works only if NOI growth, paydown, or a better exit closes the gap.
- [ ] The lender will almost certainly refuse to make this loan at all. :: At a debt service coverage of about 1.28x ($104,000 / $80,945) this loan is actually within what many lenders will do. Negative leverage is about economics, not loan approval.
- [ ] Leverage is helping you, because your return on equity beats the interest rate on the loan. :: Your year-one cash-on-cash is 3.9%, below both the cap rate and the interest rate. Leverage is not helping yet.

> Compare the debt constant to the cap rate before you borrow. If the constant is higher, you're buying a growth bet, and that's fine as long as you know you are.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** A broker sends you a $2,000,000 building with $100,000 of NOI (a 5.0% cap rate). The pro forma assumes a 70% loan at 7%, 6% annual rent growth, and a sale in year five at a 5.0% cap. The projected IRR is 14%. The market's recent rent growth has been closer to 2.5%, and similar buildings are trading around a 5.75% cap.

What is your best first move?

- [ ] Accept the 14% IRR, since the broker's model uses a standard five-year hold and a typical loan. :: A standard hold period says nothing about whether the assumptions are reasonable. The 14% depends on growth more than double the recent trend and an exit at a cap rate the market isn't currently paying.
- [x] Rerun it at 2.5% growth and a 5.75% to 6.25% exit cap. :: A downside case built from observed market data tells you how much of the return was the broker's assumptions. If it survives, you have a deal; if it doesn't, you've avoided a bet.
- [ ] Negotiate the price down by the gap between the broker's IRR and the return you require. :: Price is a tool, but you can't set it until you know the true return. Haircutting by an arbitrary figure skips the step of finding out how fragile the numbers are.
- [ ] Ask the broker to find a higher loan-to-value so the IRR rises toward your target. :: More leverage on a 5% cap with 7% debt deepens negative leverage. It raises risk without changing the underlying economics.

> The broker's pro forma is a sales document. Your job is to find out what it takes to make it true, and what happens when it isn't.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

Which of the following is NOT subtracted when computing a property's net operating income, but IS real cash you will pay while you own it?

- [ ] Property taxes, which are assessed against the building every year :: Property taxes are an operating expense and are already inside NOI. They're one of the biggest lines in the statement.
- [ ] Management fees, whether paid to a third party or charged for your own time :: Management fees (whether you pay a third party or charge for your own time) belong in operating expenses and reduce NOI.
- [x] Mortgage payments :: Debt service is below the NOI line. It's real cash out the door, which is why cash flow (NOI less debt service) and NOI are different numbers.
- [ ] Insurance premiums on the building and its liability coverage :: Insurance is an operating expense and sits inside NOI.

> NOI describes the property. Cash flow describes your deal with the lender. Keep them separate in your head.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** You own Maple Court with $592,000 of equity invested, a $1,040,000 loan, and a 6.5% cap rate at purchase. Rents haven't changed ($104,000 NOI) but market cap rates move to 7.5%. Ignore sale costs and the small amount of principal you've paid down.

Roughly how much did your equity value drop, and as a share of your invested equity?

- [ ] About $16,000, or 3%, because rents didn't change and so the value shouldn't either. :: This confuses the income, which is unchanged, with the value of that income. Price depends on what a buyer will pay per dollar of NOI.
- [ ] About $160,000, or 27%, because the cap rate moved by roughly 10% of the price. :: A one-point move from 6.5% to 7.5% isn't 10% of anything useful. The value effect is calculated by dividing the same NOI by each cap rate.
- [x] About $213,000, or 36% ($1,386,667 vs. $1,600,000). :: A $213,333 drop in value comes straight out of equity because the loan balance doesn't change. $213,333 / $592,000 is roughly 36%.
- [ ] About $213,000, or 13%, since the building itself lost 13% of its value. :: The dollar figure is right but the percentage is measured against the wrong base. The loss is borne by your $592,000 of equity, not the whole $1.6M.

> When you borrow 65% of a price, a 13% drop in value is a 36% drop in your capital. Leverage doesn't change the building. It changes who feels the move.

## Q5 | multiple_choice

Two apartment buildings both rent at exactly 1% of their purchase price per month. One is in a state with low property taxes and cheap insurance, the other in a high-tax, high-insurance metro. Why is the 1% rule a poor guide to which is the better buy?

- [x] It ignores expenses, and taxes and insurance swing NOI. :: Rent-to-price says nothing about the cost of running the property. Two buildings with the same rent can have NOI that differs by 30% or more because of taxes, insurance, and utilities.
- [ ] Because rent is not a reliable number to build any projection on. :: Rent is usually the most knowable number on the statement. The problem with the rule is what it leaves out, not that rent is uncertain.
- [ ] Because the 1% rule only applies to single-family homes, not apartment buildings. :: It started with single-family rentals but investors apply it to small apartment buildings too. The flaw is the same in both: it ignores expenses.
- [ ] Because lenders do not look at rent when sizing or approving a loan. :: Lenders underwrite NOI built from rent. The rule fails because it is too crude, not because rent is irrelevant.

> Use shortcuts to decide what to look at. Use NOI to decide what to buy.
