# Lesson

In the middle of a boom I knew a buyer I'll call Marcus. He'd read that a certain Sunbelt metro was adding 90,000 jobs a year, and he bought a 60-unit building in a suburb that had every headline going for it. What the headlines left out was that 4,100 new apartments were under construction within a ten-minute drive of his building. Every one of those new buildings was offering six to eight weeks of free rent to fill up. Marcus's tenants did the sensible thing: they moved to the shiny new place for the same money. His occupancy fell from 94% to 81% in nine months, and he spent two years offering concessions on a building he'd underwritten for rent growth.

Nothing about the job numbers was wrong. Marcus had read the demand side of the story and never opened the supply side. He bought a metro. He should have bought a submarket, and he should have looked at the cranes.

## Start with the submarket, not the city

A "market" is a pile of submarkets that behave differently. A metro of three million people might have a submarket where rents grow 6% a year and another ten miles away where they fall. The things that make a submarket move are local: the commute, the school boundary, the new employer, the highway interchange, the zoning that does or doesn't allow more buildings.

Your question is never "is this a good city?" It's "will this particular building, within this particular radius, find tenants at rents that cover my costs and rise over my hold period?" Study the metro to understand the tide. Study the submarket to understand your boat.

## Demand: who is going to rent from you, and how do you know?

Demand for housing comes from household formation, which comes from jobs, wages, and population. Demand for commercial space comes from business formation and employment. So start with the engines:

- **Job growth, and the diversity of it.** One employer providing 15% of local jobs is a concentration risk. I've watched a town with a single plant lose 40% of its renter base when the plant closed. Ask: what are the top five employers, and what happens to my occupancy if the biggest one leaves?
- **Wage growth relative to rent growth.** Rents can only rise as fast as tenants' ability to pay. When rents grow 8% a year and wages grow 3%, the music stops, and it stops fastest in the cheapest buildings.
- **Net migration and household formation.** Are people moving in, or are they leaving for somewhere cheaper? Look at moving-truck patterns, school enrollment, driver's license transfers. Public data lags, but the direction is usually clear.
- **Renter share and demographics.** Young renters with rising incomes, empty nesters downsizing, and workforce housing near a hospital are three different tenants with three different durations of stay.

## Supply: the part everyone skips

Supply is how you tell a growing market from an overbuilt one. Look for the pipeline in three stages: units *under construction*, units *permitted but not started*, and units *planned or entitled*. Only the first is certain, so start there.

The number to compute is pipeline as a percentage of existing stock, and then in **years of absorption**: how many units the market has absorbed a year (new households filling units) compared with what's coming. A pipeline of under 3% of stock in a healthy market is normal. Above 8% to 10%, expect concessions. And if the pipeline equals two or more years of absorption, you're in the game of watching whether demand outruns it.

Constraint matters too. In a place where it takes four years and a lawsuit to build anything, existing buildings are protected from competition, and it's why investors pay coastal cap rates. In a place with open land and easy permits, any rent increase invites a new building within eighteen months. That is why Sunbelt rent growth has tended to be boom-and-bust while old-industrial cities with tight zoning plod along steadily.

## Affordability: the ceiling over everything

Here's a rule so useful I'd put it on the wall: **rent-to-income**. Take the typical renter household's gross income, divide by twelve, and compare to rent. Most households can stretch to about 30% of gross income for housing before they start doubling up, moving out, or paying late. If your market is at 27%, there's room to grow. At 34% to 35%, it's capped, and rent growth will depend on wages, not on anything you do.

Effective rent matters as much as asking rent. A building asking $1,450 but giving six weeks free on a 12-month lease actually collects $1,269 a month. When you read a rent survey, check whether it's asking or effective. Concessions are the first thing to show up when a market weakens, long before the average rent line moves.

## Walk it, drive it, call it

Data tells you what happened last quarter. Your feet tell you what's happening now. For any market I intend to put real money into, I do these things, and I'd have you do them too:

1. **Drive the submarket at 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. on a weekday.** Which lots are full? Are there cars in the parking lot of the building across the street? Are the lights on?
2. **Call five leasing offices as a prospect.** Ask what's available, what the rent is, what specials they're offering, and what their turn time is. People tell you the truth when they think you're a renter. "We're offering two months free" is worth more than any survey.
3. **Count the cranes and the vacant land.** Then check the city planning department's permit list for what's coming.
4. **Talk to a property manager who owns nothing in the deal.** Ask what's changed in the last year. They know which streets are slipping.
5. **Look at the competition's online reviews.** The issues a competitor can't fix (noise, parking, safety) are your leasing advantage or your problem.

## How a cycle actually feels from the inside

Every apartment market runs a loop. Early in a recovery, little has been built for two or three years, vacancy is tight, rents jump 5% to 8%, and lenders are still nervous. That's the best time to buy and the hardest, because it feels uncomfortable. Mid-cycle, rents are strong, construction loans flow again, and cap rates compress. Late in the cycle, deliveries arrive in a wave: a garden-style building takes roughly 18 to 30 months from permit to lease-up, a mid-rise 30 to 48, so a lender's enthusiasm in year one becomes a concession in year three. Then demand stalls, concessions show up, owners with floating-rate loans run out of runway, and the cycle resets.

Where do people get hurt? Almost always the same way: they buy in year two or three of a boom, underwrite the rent growth they just watched, and hold a loan that comes due in year five or six, right when the supply from other people's year-two decisions delivers. I've seen the same pattern in three separate cycles, in three different decades, and in each case the buyers insisted "this time the jobs are different." Jobs mattered. Supply mattered more.

## A scorecard you can actually use

Don't try to forecast. Score the submarket on a handful of things you can verify, and treat any red as something your price and reserves must pay for.

| Signal | Green | Yellow | Red |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pipeline as % of existing stock | under 3% | 3% to 8% | over 8% |
| Pipeline in years of absorption | under 1 year | 1 to 2 years | over 2 years |
| Rent-to-income (renter households) | under 28% | 28% to 32% | over 32% |
| Largest employer's share of local jobs | under 5% | 5% to 10% | over 10% |
| Concessions at competing buildings | none | under 2 weeks | 4 weeks or more |

Eastgate (9% pipeline is red, 1.7 years of absorption is yellow, a 4% largest employer is green) is a supply problem. Northfield (11% employer concentration and 34% rent-to-income are both red) is a demand problem. You'll see that in the worked examples below. The point of the scorecard isn't to produce a score. It's to force you to look at every column instead of falling in love with one.

## The mistakes I see most

**Chasing last year's winners.** By the time a market has been written up in every investor newsletter, the cap rates have compressed and the construction is under way. The returns you read about were earned by the people who bought three years earlier.

**Reading demand without supply.** Marcus's mistake. Every great job number is also an invitation to developers.

**Trusting the broker's market summary.** Brokers publish market reports to help sell, and they're not wrong exactly, but they are curated. If the report is bullish, ask what's *not* in it.

**Underwriting asking rent as if it were effective rent.** In a softening market, asking rents hold for a long time while concessions quietly do the work. Check effective rents.

**Ignoring landlord-tenant law and tax regimes.** Eviction timelines, rent control or rent stabilization, and property tax reassessment rules vary hugely. A state where an eviction takes three weeks and one where it takes four months are different businesses. Ask a local attorney or a seasoned local manager before you close.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Invest where you can see, or where you can trust someone who can.** I've done deals in places I'd never lived, but never without a manager I'd checked three times. If you're buying remote, your manager is the market. Spend more time choosing them than choosing the building.

**Primary or secondary market?** A major gateway city offers liquidity (buyers always exist) and lower cap rates. A secondary market offers higher cap rates and higher risk of getting stuck when sentiment turns. If you can survive a slow sale, secondary markets pay you for that risk. If you might need to sell on a schedule, pay for the liquidity.

**The "path of growth" story.** "The city is growing this direction" has made and ruined a lot of people. Growth usually continues, but the path is not always where the investor's building is. Check the road projects, the utility extensions, and the zoning. Growth you can document is better than growth you can imagine.

## If you remember nothing else

- Underwrite a submarket, not a city. Count the supply as carefully as you count the jobs.
- Rent is capped by income. Rent-to-income near 30% means room; above 34% means wages, not you, set the next increase.
- Ask for effective rent, not asking rent, and call the leasing offices yourself.

## This week

Choose a submarket you might buy in. Find the number of units under construction (the planning department or a local brokerage report will have it) and the existing stock. Compute the pipeline percentage. Then call three leasing offices and write down the asking rent and any concession they quote.

# Example 1: Two Submarkets, Two Different Risks

## The setup

You're comparing two submarkets in the same metro for a 40-unit workforce apartment purchase. Eastgate is the one with the exciting headlines; Northfield is the quiet one that no one is writing about. You pull the data.

## The numbers

| Metric | Eastgate | Northfield |
|---|---:|---:|
| Existing apartment units | 6,000 | 4,200 |
| Units under construction | 540 | 130 |
| Pipeline as % of stock | 9.0% | 3.1% |
| Annual absorption (3-year average) | 310 units | 120 units |
| Pipeline in years of absorption | 1.7 years | 1.1 years |
| Vacancy | 7.5% | 5.2% |
| Largest employer's share of local jobs | 4% | 11% |

## What the veteran sees

Eastgate: the pipeline is 9% of stock and takes 1.7 years of demand to fill. Expect concessions at the new buildings, which pull tenants out of older ones, and vacancy likely drifting up from 7.5%. The jobs are diversified (largest employer is 4%), so the demand side is durable. The risk is supply-driven and temporary: if you can underwrite flat rents for 18 to 24 months and hold, you'll likely do well when the wave is absorbed.

Northfield: supply is light and vacancy is tight, which looks great. But one employer provides 11% of jobs, and the market's demand has been steady at 120 units a year. The risk here is concentration, and it's a binary one. Nothing will happen until something does.

Neither is better. They are different risks, and your reserves, your hold period, and your stomach for each decide the answer. If you must pick one for a five-year hold with limited reserves, Northfield's risk is a possible sharp event, and Eastgate's is a slow squeeze. I'd generally prefer the slow squeeze with diversified demand, because I can plan for it.

## Change one thing

Say the Northfield employer announces a 20% workforce cut: that's about 2.2% of local jobs. If households in the market shrink proportionally, you might lose 90 to 120 units of demand: roughly a year's worth of absorption, on a market that only has 130 units coming. The vacancy goes from 5.2% to something like 7.5%, and the building you bought at a tight-market price is underwater against the rent growth you assumed.

# Example 2: The Ceiling and the Concession

## The setup

Same two submarkets. You now test whether rent growth has room to run, using renter household income and effective rent.

## The numbers

| Metric | Eastgate | Northfield |
|---|---:|---:|
| Average in-place rent | $1,215 | $1,490 |
| Median renter household income | $54,000 | $52,000 |
| Rent-to-income | 27.0% | 34.4% |
| Rent at 30% of income | $1,350 | $1,300 |
| Headroom to 30% | +$135 (+11%) | -$190 (-13%) |

Rent-to-income = rent × 12 / income. For Eastgate: $1,215 × 12 = $14,580; $14,580 / $54,000 = 27.0%.

Now the concession. A new Eastgate building asks **$1,450** and is offering six weeks (1.5 months) free on a 12-month lease. Effective rent = $1,450 × (12 - 1.5) / 12 = **$1,268.75**.

## What the veteran sees

Eastgate has room: 11% of headroom to the 30% line. But the new buildings are asking $1,450 and effectively collecting $1,269, which is only $54 a month more than the older building at $1,215. That means the new supply is competing directly with your older building, and it explains why your own rent growth may stall even though affordability is fine. In practice, the effective-rent gap is what a tenant weighs, so any renovated unit you create will need to beat $1,269, not $1,450.

Northfield is already stretched. At 34.4% of income, tenants have no room to absorb increases, and rent growth there will track wage growth: probably 2% to 3%, not the 5% to 6% a broker might promise. Those tenants also tend to be more sensitive to unexpected costs, such as a utility bill pass-through.

## Change one thing

If renter incomes in Northfield rise 4% a year while rents hold flat, how long to reach the 30% line? Income: $52,000 × 1.04^n. You need $1,490 × 12 / 0.30 = $59,600 of income. $59,600 / $52,000 = 1.146, and 1.04^n = 1.146 gives n ≈ 3.5 years. Rents in Northfield could stay roughly flat for three and a half years before the market is "healthy" again by this test. If your business plan needs 5% rent growth in the first two years, it needs a different market.

# Example 3: Testing the Broker's Rent Claim

## The setup

You're underwriting Maple Court (16 units, 2BR/1BA, 850 sf, in-place average rent $1,075). The broker's offering memorandum says "market rents are $1,250, so there's $175 a month of upside on every unit." You pull five comparable buildings and adjust each rent to match Maple Court's condition and amenities.

## The numbers

| Comp | Quoted rent | Net adjustment | Adjusted rent | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| A | $1,225 | -$175 | $1,050 | Renovated, in-unit laundry |
| B | $1,140 | -$50 | $1,090 | Newer appliances |
| C | $1,095 | +$25 | $1,120 | Worse location, no parking |
| D | $1,310 | -$210 | $1,100 | Newer building, pool, gym |
| E | $1,080 | $0 | $1,080 | Very similar |
| **Average** | | | **$1,088** | Median $1,090 |

Subject in-place rent: $1,075. Loss-to-lease = $1,088 - $1,075 = **$13/month** per unit.

| Upside | Per year | Value at a 6.5% cap |
|---|---:|---:|
| Broker's claim: $175 × 16 units × 12 | $33,600 | $516,923 |
| Comp-supported: $13 × 16 units × 12 | $2,496 | $38,400 |

## What the veteran sees

The broker's $1,250 isn't a fiction: Comp D really does ask $1,310. But it's a newer building with a pool and a gym, and an unadjusted comp is a sales tool. After adjusting for what each comp offers that Maple Court doesn't, the rent supported by the market is about $1,088. The "upside" shrinks from $33,600 a year to $2,496.

At a 6.5% cap rate, the difference is $478,000 of value. If you'd paid for the broker's rents, you'd have paid roughly a half-million dollars for income that doesn't exist. To earn rent above $1,088 you'd have to spend money on renovation (Module 9) and prove the premium, unit by unit.

## Change one thing

Suppose you do renovate and the market pays a $100 premium for renovated units, on top of the $1,088. That's a rent of $1,188, still $62 under the broker's $1,250. The honest value-add story is a story about what renovation earns, not about "market rents" you read in a brochure.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

A submarket has 5,000 existing apartments, 450 under construction, and has absorbed about 200 units a year on average. What is the right read of the supply pipeline?

- [ ] The pipeline is about 4.5% of stock, so supply is light. :: That figure mixes up the numbers. 450 divided by 5,000 is 9%, not 4.5%, and 9% is a heavy pipeline, not a light one.
- [x] It's 9% of stock and 2.25 years of absorption, so expect concessions. :: 450 / 5,000 = 9% and 450 / 200 = 2.25 years of demand needed to fill it. That combination usually shows up as free rent at new buildings, which pulls tenants out of older ones.
- [ ] It's 9% of stock, which is normal, so supply is not a concern. :: Under about 3% is normal in a healthy market. At 9%, and with more than two years of absorption needed, supply is the main risk.
- [ ] You cannot read supply until the buildings have finished construction and opened. :: Under-construction units are the most certain part of the pipeline. Waiting until they open means waiting until the concessions have already hit your rent roll.

> Count the cranes, then divide by what the market actually absorbs. Supply tells you where concessions are coming from before the rent survey does.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** You're looking at a 30-unit building asking $1,380 a month on average. Median renter household income in the submarket is $50,400. The broker's pro forma assumes 5% rent growth for each of the next four years, justified by "strong demand and low vacancy." You've also noticed that wages in the area have been growing about 3% a year.

How should you treat the broker's rent growth assumption?

- [ ] Accept it, since low vacancy means the broker's growth is well supported. :: Low vacancy is a sign of recent demand, not of future ability to pay. At $1,380 renters are already near the affordability ceiling.
- [x] Tie it to wages near 3%, since rent is already ~33% of income. :: $1,380 × 12 = $16,560, and $16,560 / $50,400 = 32.9% of gross income, above the roughly 30% comfort level. With little headroom, rent growth can't outrun wage growth for long.
- [ ] Reject the deal outright because rent-to-income exceeds 30%. :: A rent-to-income ratio above 30% is a warning that growth will be limited, not a reason to walk. A deal can still work on flat or modest rent growth if the price reflects it.
- [ ] Assume 5% growth in years one and two and 3% afterward as a compromise. :: Splitting the difference is arbitrary. The affordability data says growth beyond wages is unlikely at all, so front-loading 5% builds in the least supportable years first.

> Rent is capped by income. When rent-to-income is already high, price the deal on wage growth and treat anything more as a gift.

## Q3 | multiple_choice

A competing building advertises an asking rent of $1,500 and offers one month free on a 12-month lease. What is the effective monthly rent a tenant actually pays?

- [ ] $1,500 :: That is the asking rent. A month free means the tenant pays rent for only 11 of 12 months, so the average falls.
- [ ] $1,450 :: This is the result of subtracting $50 for no clear reason. The concession is one full month, $1,500, spread across the 12 months of the lease.
- [x] $1,375 :: $1,500 × 11 / 12 = $1,375. Over the lease the tenant pays $16,500, or $1,375 a month on average. That's the number your own building competes against.
- [ ] $1,250 :: This subtracts the free month from the monthly rent as if it applied every month. The concession is a one-time $1,500 across the full 12-month lease, not a $250 monthly discount.

> Always compare effective rent to effective rent. Concessions are the first sign a market is softening.

## Q4 | scenario

**Scenario.** A broker is pitching a 24-unit building in an up-and-coming area. The asking price is 15% above recent comparable sales. Her argument: the state has "planned" an interchange on the nearby highway that will bring new employers within five years. You've checked, and the interchange appears in a long-range plan but is not yet funded or scheduled for construction.

What is your best response?

- [ ] Pay the asking price, because early buyers in growth corridors earn the most. :: Early buyers also lose the most when the growth doesn't arrive or arrives late. Paying a 15% premium for an unfunded project turns a possibility into a certain cost.
- [x] Offer at comp-based value and treat the interchange as upside. :: Price what the income and comparable sales support today. If the interchange gets funded, you collect the gain; if not, you haven't paid for it.
- [ ] Walk away from any area where the growth story depends on a government project. :: That's too blunt. Infrastructure often does drive real growth. The error is paying in advance for it, not noticing it.
- [ ] Split the difference and offer 7.5% above comps. :: This values a speculative event at a number picked for negotiating comfort. If the event is unfunded, the right premium is closer to zero than to half the seller's ask.

> Growth you can document, such as funded and under construction, is worth paying for. Growth you can only imagine is worth owning an option on, not paying a premium for.

## Q5 | multiple_choice

You want to know whether competing buildings are quietly offering concessions. Which source is the most reliable?

- [ ] The broker's market report on rents and vacancy for the submarket :: Broker reports tend to report asking rents and are written to support a sale, so they often lag or omit concessions.
- [x] Calling several leasing offices as a prospective renter :: Leasing agents tell prospects what specials are running, what the real availability is, and how long units take to lease. It's current, direct, and nearly free.
- [ ] The listed asking rents on apartment websites for those buildings :: Asking rents are the number concessions are taken from, so they hide the discount. They show what a building wants, not what it collects.
- [ ] The prior year's average rent growth figure from a national data provider :: This is backward-looking and aggregated across a whole metro, so it cannot show what a specific competitor is offering this month.

> Data tells you what happened. A five-minute phone call tells you what's happening.
