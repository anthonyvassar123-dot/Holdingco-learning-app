# Lesson

I once owned a 48-unit building managed by a firm that sent me beautiful monthly reports: 96% occupied, collections right on budget, NOI tracking the pro forma. I believed them for fourteen months. Then a tenant called the number on my sign to complain about a leak, because she couldn't reach anyone at the management office, and she mentioned that her neighbor in 2C had moved out in the spring. 2C was on the report as occupied. So were three other units. The manager was reporting vacant units as occupied, collecting nothing on them, and backfilling the income from the security deposit account. When I finally compared twelve months of bank deposits to the reports, the building was 88% occupied, not 96%, and the account that should have held $41,000 in deposits held $9,000.

I got most of it back, mostly from the manager's insurance and a settlement. I never again let a monthly report be the only thing between me and a building. That experience is the root of this module's main idea: operations is where your returns are earned or lost every day, and the person doing the operating is not you. Your job is to design the system that keeps them honest and the building healthy.

## What operations actually is

Operations is everything that happens between the closing and the sale: leasing units, collecting rent, answering maintenance requests, turning units, paying bills, keeping the books, handling the tenants, keeping the building legal. In a stabilized apartment building it is also where about 10% to 20% of NOI is determined by how well you do the basics. A well-run building and a poorly run building of the same size, in the same neighborhood, will differ by several points of occupancy, a couple of points of bad debt, and 5% to 10% of expenses. At a $672,000 income line, those few points are $30,000 to $60,000 a year, and at a 6.5% cap rate, $450,000 to $900,000 of value.

## Self-manage or hire?

Self-management saves the fee and puts you close to the building. It costs your time, usually more than you expect. A 40-unit building takes 10 to 20 hours a week when things are calm and 40 hours when they're not: leasing calls, maintenance coordination, vendor management, rent collection, eviction paperwork, bookkeeping. Hire a manager when any of these is true:

- The value of your time (what you'd earn doing something else with it) is above the cost of the manager.
- The building is more than a short drive away.
- You have more than one property, or a day job that doesn't bend.
- You lack the temperament for confrontations about late rent and for 2 a.m. calls.
- You plan to scale, and a systematized operation is the foundation.

Remember too that lenders and buyers will underwrite a management fee whether you pay one or not. The market treats "free" owner management as a cost that's simply been deferred. And if you ever plan to sell, a building that depends on you personally is harder to sell than one with documented systems and a manager in place.

## What management costs

For apartments in the mid-2020s, a third-party manager typically charges:

- **A management fee:** 4% to 8% of collected revenue; smaller buildings sit at the high end, larger ones at the low end. Some set a monthly minimum.
- **Leasing fees:** often 25% to 100% of one month's rent per new lease, sometimes bundled into the base fee.
- **Construction management fees:** 3% to 8% of renovation spending, when they supervise projects.
- **Payroll reimbursement:** on-site staff are usually employees of the property, and their wages and benefits are charged to the owner on top of the fee.
- **Extras:** lease renewal fees, eviction fees, set-up fees, technology fees, mark-ups on maintenance. Read the contract line by line.

On Cedar Ridge (40 units, $672,240 of collected income), a 5% fee is $33,612, and leasing fees at half a month's rent for 16 new leases are another $11,600. That's $45,212, or 6.7% of income, before payroll. Compare that number to what the manager earns you, and to what your own time is worth. Both comparisons are in Example 1.

## Managing your manager

You can delegate the work, but not the responsibility. The structure I use with every third-party manager:

1. **A written management agreement** with a term of one year, a 30-day termination right without cause, and no auto-renewing multi-year lock-in. Define the fee schedule, the budget approval process, and the spending authority, usually $500 to $1,500 per item without calling you, with emergency exceptions.
2. **Bank accounts in your name.** The operating account and the security deposit account should be titled to the property or your entity, with you as a signer or at least with read-only online access. Never let funds sit in the manager's commingled trust account without independent visibility.
3. **A monthly package** that includes the rent roll, income statement, balance sheet, bank statement and reconciliation, delinquency report, aged payables, and a vacancy and leasing report. You read the numbers; you compare the rent roll to the bank deposits.
4. **An annual budget** that you approve, with line-item variance explanations each month for anything over 5% or $1,000.
5. **Key performance indicators** tracked every month: economic occupancy, physical occupancy, delinquency as a percent of billed rent, turnover rate, days vacant per turn, work order response and completion time, and renewal rate.
6. **An independent look at least twice a year.** Walk the property unannounced. Read a few tenant reviews. Call two tenants and ask how the building is run. Check the vacancies in person.

## Turnover: the silent drain

Every time a tenant leaves, you pay: make-ready costs, lost rent while the unit is empty, marketing and leasing costs, and the manager's attention. A turn on a $1,300 unit commonly costs $3,000 to $4,500 in total. In Example 2, we price one at $3,583, and then you see what that means for retention efforts.

Retention is the cheapest source of value in operations. A renewing tenant costs you almost nothing: a lease form and, perhaps, a $300 renewal incentive. A turning tenant costs you ten times that. Fast maintenance response, clear communication, honest renewal pricing (many owners raise rents by the maximum, then wonder why half the building leaves), and a clean, well-kept building all reduce turnover. The relationship between a tenant and the building is built in small events: how quickly the dishwasher leak was fixed, whether the hallway light was replaced.

## Collections: the cost of waiting

Delinquency starts small and compounds. A tenant who is a week late in March is a month late in April, and by June you're in court. The cost isn't only the unpaid rent: it's legal fees, the vacancy while the eviction runs, the turnover cost, and a unit that may be returned damaged. Example 3 prices a typical eviction at $8,683 on a $1,300 unit.

The cure is process, applied consistently:

- **Screening before move-in.** Verify income (commonly 3× rent), employment, rental history, and credit using written criteria applied the same way to every applicant. Fair housing laws (federal, state, and local) forbid discrimination in housing, and the screening criteria and the leasing process must comply with them. Have an attorney review your standards and train anyone who talks to applicants.
- **A firm, uniform, polite payment policy** with a clear due date, a grace period if you offer one, a late-fee schedule that complies with local limits, and a standard notice timeline.
- **Early contact.** A call on day 3 and a written notice on day 5 resolves more problems than any court filing. Payment plans for first-time lapses, in writing, often cost less than eviction.
- **Knowing the local process.** Eviction timelines range from three weeks to six months depending on jurisdiction. If you're buying in a slow jurisdiction, underwrite higher bad debt and tighter screening.

## Staffing and scale

Small buildings have a problem big buildings don't: fixed costs are spread across fewer units. A full-time on-site manager at $48,000 is $1,200 per unit per year on a 40-unit building but about $480 per unit on a 100-unit building. That's why many 20- to 50-unit properties use a part-time or shared on-site person and a central manager, and why expense ratios fall as buildings get larger. As a rule of thumb, one on-site manager can run 75 to 125 units, and one maintenance technician 50 to 75. If a building is too small for dedicated staff, consider a shared-services model across several properties or a handyman on retainer.

## What good looks like

Numbers help you judge a manager. On a stabilized apartment building in the mid-2020s, I want to see economic occupancy of 92% to 95% or better, delinquency under 1.5% of billed rent, turnover of 40% to 50%, 18 to 28 days of vacancy per turn, routine work orders closed in two or three days, and a renewal rate above 55% to 60%. None of these is a law; they're a baseline for a conversation. If your building sits outside them, the question to ask is not "who's to blame?" but "what changed?"

## The mistakes I see most

**Trusting the report without checking the bank.** My own mistake. Always reconcile.

**Hiring a manager on the lowest fee.** A manager who charges 3% and lets occupancy drop four points costs you more than one who charges 6% and fills the building.

**Letting turnover happen by accident.** Treat every non-renewal as a failure to understand.

**Raising rents to the maximum every year.** A $75 increase that causes a $3,583 turn is a loss if it drives out a tenant who would have stayed at $50.

**Delaying maintenance.** Deferred repairs compound. A $200 leak left for six months becomes a $4,000 ceiling and a vacated unit.

**Ignoring local law.** Security deposit handling, habitability standards, notice requirements, and fair housing rules vary by state and city. Get a local landlord-tenant attorney's review of your lease and notices before you need it.

## Judgment calls the textbooks skip

**Your first manager hire is a bet on a person, not a firm.** A firm's reputation helps, but the person assigned to your building decides whether it runs well. Ask who will run your property, how many properties they run, and speak to that person.

**When to replace the manager.** If you've raised the same issue twice and not seen change, or you find a number that doesn't reconcile and the explanation doesn't hold, start looking. The cost of a bad manager is almost never the fee.

**What not to optimize.** Cutting maintenance, payroll, or turn quality to hit a budget feels like an NOI gain. It's usually a loan against next year's occupancy.

## If you remember nothing else

- Reconcile the manager's reports to the bank. Own the accounts.
- A turn costs $3,000 to $4,500. Retention is the cheapest value you can create.
- Collect early and consistently. Eviction is the most expensive way to solve a late-rent problem.

# Example 1: What the Fee Buys

## The setup

Cedar Ridge is a 40-unit building with collected income of $672,240, gross potential rent of $696,000 and other income of $18,000. A third-party manager charges 5% of collections plus half a month's rent per new lease. You are choosing between hiring them and managing it yourself. The manager says they can lift occupancy by 3 points, cut bad debt by 2% of collected income, and shorten the time to re-lease a vacant unit by 10 days (on 16 turns a year at $1,450 rent).

## The numbers

| Cost of third-party management | Amount |
|---|---:|
| 5% fee ($672,240 × 0.05) | $33,612 |
| Leasing fees (16 leases × 0.5 × $1,450) | $11,600 |
| **Total** | **$45,212** (6.7% of income) |

| What the manager might improve | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Occupancy +3 points (3% × $696,000) | $20,880 |
| Bad debt reduced by 2% of collected income | $13,445 |
| 10 fewer days vacant per turn (16 × 10 × $1,450 / 30) | $7,733 |
| **Total improvement** | **$42,058** |

Net of fees: $42,058 - $45,212 = **-$3,154**.

Your time if self-managing: 15 hours a week × 52 = 780 hours. At $75 an hour that's $58,500. The break-even value of your time is $45,212 / 780 = **$58 an hour**.

## What the veteran sees

On pure performance, the manager roughly breaks even; they don't make you rich. That's an honest result, and it's what most owners find on a stable building of this size. The decision usually turns on two other things: what your hours are worth to you, and the protection the manager's systems and size provide.

If your time is worth less than $58 an hour to you, you might as well manage the building yourself. If it's worth more, which is true of most people with professional jobs, hiring is cheaper than it looks. And if you're unwilling or unable to do the work consistently, a competent manager protects the downside: an owner who drifts can easily lose more than the fee to a few points of occupancy.

## Change one thing

Suppose the manager's improvements are only half what they promised: $21,029. Net of fees you're now $24,183 worse off than self-managing at zero cost for your time. That is the downside of paying for performance that doesn't arrive. It is why the agreement should have a clear termination right and why you should look at the KPIs every month. A manager should be hired for a result, and fired for its absence.

# Example 2: The Price of a Turn

## The setup

You own a 40-unit building with an average rent of $1,300. Your turnover rate has crept to 55% a year. You're considering a retention program: a $300 gift card at each lease renewal, plus a commitment to complete maintenance requests within 48 hours. You estimate it would bring turnover down to 45%.

## The numbers

Cost of one turn (annual figures below use the unrounded $3,583.33):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cleaning | $350 |
| Paint | $600 |
| Flooring repair | $550 |
| Other repairs | $350 |
| Leasing and marketing (half a month's rent) | $650 |
| Vacancy: 25 days × ($1,300 / 30) | $1,083 |
| **Total per turn** | **$3,583** |

| Turnover rate | Turns per year | Annual turnover cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| 40% | 16 | $57,333 |
| 55% | 22 | $78,833 |
| 45% | 18 | $64,500 |

The retention program: at 45% turnover, 22 tenants renew, each receiving $300: cost **$6,600**. It prevents (55% - 45%) × 40 = 4 turns: savings **$14,333**. Net benefit: **$7,733** a year.

The break-even: a $300 gift pays for itself if it prevents one turn for every 12 gifts ($3,583 / $300 = 11.9).

## What the veteran sees

Going from 40% to 55% turnover cost $21,500 a year, and most owners never see that on a line item because it's spread across make-ready, vacancy, and leasing. The retention program costs $6,600 and saves more than twice that if it works as projected. Even if it works only half as well (preventing 2 turns), it saves $7,167 and costs $6,600: roughly break-even, with the other benefits (a more stable community, fewer vacant weeks) as a bonus.

The more important point is the cause: why did turnover rise from 40% to 55%? A gift card doesn't fix slow repairs, a leaking roof, or a rent increase that exceeded what tenants thought fair. The program works best as a signal, and it should come with a look at the underlying reasons. Ask departing tenants why they left.

## Change one thing

You raised renewal rents by $90 a month on average, versus a $50 increase that tenants would have accepted. The extra $40 a month on 22 renewing tenants earns $10,560 a year. If the larger increase pushes out 3 additional tenants, that costs 3 × $3,583 = $10,750. The extra increase is a wash at best, and it would be a loss with a fourth departure. Pricing for retention often beats pricing for the maximum.

# Example 3: What Delinquency Costs

## The setup

On the same 40-unit building, four tenants fall behind during the year. You are comparing two collection policies. Under the passive policy, you wait and send letters. Under the active policy, you call on day 3, serve notice on day 5, and offer a written payment plan to first-time cases.

## The numbers

The cost of a full eviction on a $1,300 unit:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Unpaid rent for three months | $3,900 |
| Legal fees and court costs | $1,200 |
| Turnover cost (from Example 2) | $3,583 |
| **Total** | **$8,683** |

| Policy | Outcome per 4 delinquent tenants | Annual cost |
|---|---|---:|
| Passive | All four go three months unpaid and are evicted | 4 × $8,683 = **$34,733** |
| Active | Two cure within the month, losing about one month's rent each; the other two are evicted | 2 × $1,300 + 2 × $8,683 = **$19,967** |

Savings from the active policy: $34,733 - $19,967 = **$14,766**. If you assume three of four cure early (a stricter, earlier process), the active policy costs 3 × $1,300 + $8,683 = $12,583, and saves **$22,150**.

For scale, 1% of collected income on this building is $6,722. The passive policy's $34,733 is 5.2% of income.

## What the veteran sees

The passive policy feels gentler, and it's the most expensive thing you can do. Each month you wait adds $1,300 to the loss without improving the odds of repayment. The active policy loses less because it ends the problem earlier, whether by payment or by departure.

This is also where consistency matters. Treat every tenant the same way, with the same notices on the same timeline, and document everything. Inconsistent enforcement is how a collections process turns into a fair housing complaint. Write the policy down, give it to your manager, and have an attorney review it.

## Change one thing

You buy in a jurisdiction where evictions take six months, not three. The unpaid rent in a full eviction becomes $7,800, and the total cost is $12,583. Passive policy cost: about $50,300. Even active policy is expensive when it ends in court. In slow jurisdictions, the only effective tools are screening, deposits, and early intervention. Underwrite bad debt at 3% to 4% of collected income, not 1%, and be stricter on the front end.

# Quiz

## Q1 | multiple_choice

You plan to self-manage a 40-unit building, which saves the 5% fee. When you apply for a loan, the lender's underwriting deducts a management fee anyway. Why?

- [ ] Because lenders want to discourage self-management :: The lender's concern is not who manages the building; it's the income that would be available to repay the loan if a professional manager had to be hired.
- [ ] Because the fee is a legal requirement of the loan :: Lenders often require a management agreement or approval of the manager, but the deduction itself is an underwriting convention, not a legal rule.
- [x] Lenders assume a professional would have to be paid if you couldn't continue. :: The loan must be safe even if the owner is unavailable or the building is sold. Underwriting a market fee gives a realistic, transferable NOI.
- [ ] Because management fees are always tax-deductible :: Tax treatment has nothing to do with underwriting. The fee is deducted to reflect a realistic cost of operating the building.

> Value is built on income that someone else could produce. Your free labor isn't part of the number.

## Q2 | scenario

**Scenario.** A manager's fee and leasing charges would total $45,212 a year on your 40-unit building. You'd spend roughly 780 hours a year self-managing. You have a full-time job where you value your time at about $40 an hour, and the building is a 90-minute drive away. The manager's projected improvements are worth about $42,000.

What is the most reasonable decision?

- [ ] Self-manage, since $45,212 exceeds the projected improvements :: This ignores the cost of your time and the drive. At $40 an hour, 780 hours is $31,200, and the commute and the risk of neglecting the building matter further.
- [ ] Hire the cheapest manager available to maximize savings :: The lowest fee often brings the weakest performance. A cheap manager who lets occupancy slip can cost more than a better one costs in fees.
- [x] Hire a manager, with KPIs and a 30-day termination right. :: With a 90-minute drive and a day job, performance risk from self-management is high. Contractual KPIs and an easy exit make it a managed, reversible bet.
- [ ] Defer the decision until a problem appears at the building :: Operational problems compound quietly. By the time one is visible it is usually costly.

> Hire for your time and your risk, but keep the right to fire for performance.

## Q3 | scenario

**Scenario.** A unit turn costs $3,583 on your 40-unit building. Turnover is 55%. You can offer $300 to each renewing tenant and respond to maintenance within 48 hours, which you estimate lowers turnover to 45%.

Roughly what is the annual net benefit, and what does it depend on?

- [ ] About $14,333, since that is the value of four prevented turns :: This ignores the $6,600 cost of the gift cards paid to the 22 tenants who renew. Net benefit is the savings minus the cost.
- [x] About $7,733, if the program really prevents four turns. :: Four turns avoided save $14,333, minus $6,600 in incentives. If the program prevents only two turns the benefit is about break-even, so the effect is the key assumption.
- [ ] About $21,500, the full cost of the extra turnover above 40% :: That figure is the cost of the entire increase from 40% to 55%. The program only reduces turnover to 45%, and it has its own cost.
- [ ] Nothing, because tenants who leave were never going to stay :: Some leave for reasons outside your control, but many leave over service, price, or condition issues that can be addressed.

> Compare the cost of the program with the cost of the turn, and be honest about the odds it works.

## Q4 | multiple_choice

Which arrangement gives an owner the best protection against a manager misreporting income or diverting funds?

- [ ] A detailed monthly income statement prepared by the manager :: A statement prepared by the person being checked is the thing you need to verify. It is evidence of their reporting, not of the bank balance.
- [ ] A long-term contract with a respected management firm :: A strong firm lowers the risk but doesn't eliminate it. A long lock-in also makes it harder to leave if problems emerge.
- [x] Accounts in your name with view access, and reconciling reports to the bank. :: Independent visibility into deposits is the one control a manager cannot manipulate. Reconcile the rent roll and reports to actual cash every month.
- [ ] A personal guarantee from the manager's owner :: A guarantee can help you recover after the fact but doesn't help you catch the problem early.

> Trust the report, verify the bank. The second is cheap, and the first can be wrong for a long time.

## Q5 | scenario

**Scenario.** Your manager's monthly report shows 95% occupancy and collections on budget. But your three-way check shows twelve-month bank deposits 6% below what the reports imply, and a drive-by shows lights off in several units listed as occupied. The manager says the gap is "timing."

What is your best next step?

- [ ] Accept the explanation, since a 6% timing difference is normal :: Timing differences net out over twelve months. A persistent gap in annual deposits is not timing.
- [ ] Terminate the manager immediately and sue for the full 6% :: Acting before you know what happened can destroy evidence and your position. Establish the facts first.
- [x] Audit units, leases, and deposits against the rent roll, then decide. :: Verify which units are occupied, trace the deposits unit by unit, and check the security deposit account. Then you have the facts to decide on cure, termination, or recovery.
- [ ] Ignore the gap since occupancy appears fine in the reports :: The reports are the thing in question. Occupancy that can't be traced to deposits isn't established.

> When the report and the bank disagree, the bank is right. Find out why before you act.
